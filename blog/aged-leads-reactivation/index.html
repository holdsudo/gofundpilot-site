Reactivating Aged Leads and Old Applications

Your CRM already holds thousands of merchants who once asked for help. A careful reactivation plan treats them as people with changed circumstances, not a list to blast.

By the FundPilot teamUpdated October 15, 2026

  7. Questions

Every funding shop that has been around a few years is sitting on a quiet asset: merchants who inquired, applied, or even got an offer, and then went silent. A landscaper who wanted funding last spring but decided to wait. A dental office whose file stalled when the owner went on vacation. A retailer who was declined because they had only eight months in business, and now has twenty.

Most of these records sit untouched because working them feels like a long shot. In practice, old applications are often better prospects than fresh cold data, because the merchant already knows your name and some of their situation is already on file. The risk is approaching them carelessly: a blast to a stale list, with outdated consent and no real reason to call.

Here is a methodical way to bring them back to life.

Pre-flight summary

  • Check consent and opt-outs for every segment before any outreach; involve counsel.
  • Segment old records by why the deal stopped, not just by age.
  • Lead with a specific, genuine reason connected to the merchant's situation.
  • Use old notes for context but always collect fresh statements.
  • Work in waves and measure each segment separately.

Leg 01Check consent and contact permissions first

Before any call or text, look at what each merchant agreed to and when. Consent from an inquiry years ago, for a different purpose, possibly naming a different company if the data was bought, may not cover what you plan to do now. Some merchants will have opted out since; their wishes must be honored regardless of how promising the file looks.

This is the part to review with qualified counsel, especially for purchased aged data and for texting. Requirements around consent, revocation and Do Not Call lists can be specific and change over time. This is general information, not legal advice. If consent is unclear for a segment, email or a manual, one-to-one call after review may be appropriate where a dialer or text campaign is not.

  1. Pull the consent record for each segment: wording, date, source, companies named.
  2. Remove anyone on your internal do-not-contact list.
  3. Check against applicable registries per the process your counsel set.
  4. Flag records with unclear consent for review instead of outreach.
  5. Decide channels per segment based on what consent actually covers.

Leg 02Segment by why the deal stopped

Old records are not one list. The reason each deal stalled tells you what has to have changed for a conversation to be worthwhile. Tag them by stage reached and reason lost, even if you have to infer it from notes.

  • Inquired but never reached: often just a timing problem on both sides.
  • Applied, never sent statements: the merchant may have been unsure or busy.
  • Declined for time in business or revenue: worth revisiting once enough time has passed.
  • Received offers and declined them: the terms may not have fit; ask what would have.
  • Funded elsewhere: may be relevant later, with careful, responsible handling of existing positions.
  • Funded with you and paid off or nearly done: a renewal conversation, handled on its merits.

Leg 03Find a genuine new reason to reach out

Calling to see if anything has changed is the weakest possible opener. Give the merchant a reason that relates to their situation. For a merchant declined on time in business, the reason is simple: you mentioned you had been open eight months; you are well past a year now, and that may open different options. For a stalled application: you were looking at a second location last year, I wanted to see whether that is still on the table.

Seasonal businesses are a good example of timing. Say a pool service company inquired in autumn, when revenue was dropping. Reaching out in late winter, when they are preparing for the busy season, connects with a real need. That is a reason, not a pretext.

Do not call to check in. Call because something about their business has plausibly changed, and say what.

Leg 04Use what is already on file, carefully

Old notes and documents make the first conversation better, because you are not starting from zero. But they are also out of date. Never present an old figure as current, and never prepare a submission from stale statements. Ask for fresh ones.

In FundPilot, the AI assistant answers from live CRM data, so a broker can ask what happened on this deal and why it stalled and get a summary from the notes, calls and transcripts before dialing. When new statements arrive, statement underwriting recalculates deposits, balances, NSFs and existing positions with each figure linked to the source page, so you are judging today's business rather than last year's.

Leg 05Pace the outreach and measure it by segment

Work a reactivation in waves, not all at once. A few hundred records a week lets brokers give each conversation attention, keeps calling volume from spiking on your numbers, and lets you see which segments respond before committing more time.

FundPilot campaigns can run text, email and call sequences per segment that respect local time and stop on reply or funding. Track responses, conversations, applications and funded deals separately for each segment, because the declined-for-time-in-business group and the received-offers group will behave very differently.

Leg 06Be straight with merchants about fit

Some merchants you reach will still not be good candidates, and some will already carry funding that makes more a bad idea for them. Tell them so. Do not encourage anyone to hide existing positions or to take more than the business can carry because you have finally reached them after months of trying.

That honesty is part of why reactivation works over time. A merchant who heard a straight answer from you twice is the one who calls you first when the business is genuinely ready.

Questions from the floor

How old is too old for a lead to be worth calling?

Age alone does not decide it. A merchant who applied two years ago and was declined for time in business may be a strong prospect now, while a recent record with no real interest may not be. What matters more is whether consent still covers contact and whether something has plausibly changed in their business. Check consent with counsel before outreach.

Can we text old leads who never opted out?

Not opting out does not automatically mean current consent covers your texts. The original wording, purpose, age and which company was named all matter, and texting has its own requirements. Have qualified counsel review the consent behind each segment before texting. Treat this as general guidance rather than legal advice.

What is the best opener for a reactivation call?

One that names who you are and gives a specific reason connected to their situation. For example, referencing the expansion they mentioned last year, or noting that they now have more time in business than when they first applied. Avoid vague check-ins. Then listen; their circumstances may have changed in ways your notes cannot show.

Should we use old statements to prequalify reactivated merchants?

Use them only for background. A business's deposits, balances and positions can change a lot over months, so any assessment or submission should be based on fresh statements. Presenting stale figures to funders would misrepresent the file. Ask the merchant for recent statements, ideally through a secure upload portal.
