What an AI Assistant Should (and Shouldn't) Do for a Funding Broker

AI can take the typing off a broker's plate. It should never take the judgment, the signature or the send button. Here is where to draw the line.

It is 4:40 on a Friday. You have three merchants waiting on follow-ups, one application half-filled from a call you took at lunch, and a manager asking which of your files are still waiting on statements. None of it is hard. It is just a lot of typing and looking things up, and it is the reason good brokers leave the office later than they should.

That is the honest case for an AI assistant on a funding floor. Not a robot broker, not an autopilot that pitches merchants on its own, but a capable assistant that drafts the routine text and answers questions about your own pipeline so you can spend the saved time on calls that need a human.

The line between helpful and harmful is fairly clear once you look at it closely. This guide covers what a sensible assistant should handle, what it must never do alone, and how to tell the difference in a product demo.

Pre-flight summary

  • The best use of AI on a funding floor is drafting and answering, not deciding.
  • Answers must come from your live CRM data, not general guesses.
  • Nothing should be sent, submitted or saved without a person confirming it.
  • Approvals, rates, stacking decisions and legal questions stay with people.
  • Check where merchant data goes and whether the assistant respects role-based access.

Leg 01Good job one: answering from your actual CRM data

The most useful thing an assistant can do is answer questions about your own book accurately. Which of my deals have been in the offers stage for more than five days? Which merchants have I texted this week who have not replied? What did this restaurant owner say about their slow season on the last call?

The key word is accurately. An assistant that guesses, or answers from general knowledge about the industry, is worse than useless when you are about to call a merchant. You want answers drawn from live CRM records, call transcripts and notes, so the response reflects what actually happened on the file.

This also helps managers. Instead of building a report to find stalled deals, they can ask the question directly and then go and look at the deals themselves.

Leg 02Good job two: drafting the routine writing

Brokers write constantly: follow-up texts after a missed call, emails asking for the most recent month's statement, a summary for a lender, a recap of an offer for a merchant comparing options. Most of these follow a pattern but still need the specifics of the deal.

A good assistant drafts these from the deal record. Say a merchant told you on a recorded call that they need funds to cover a seasonal inventory order and that their partner co-owns the business. The assistant can pull that context into an application draft and a follow-up email listing the documents still outstanding, both waiting for you to read and adjust.

  • Application drafts filled from call notes and the merchant record
  • Follow-up texts and emails that reference the actual conversation
  • Plain-language summaries of a long call for a colleague taking over a file
  • Document request lists based on what is already on file and what is missing

Leg 03The hard line: a person confirms every send and save

Here is where the shouldn't part begins. An assistant should never send a message to a merchant, submit a file to a lender or change a record without a person reviewing and confirming it. That is not a technical limitation to work around. It is the design.

The reasons are practical. A draft might include a figure that is not quite right, or a phrase that overstates what you can offer. Messaging rules around texting and calling are your responsibility, not the software's. And a merchant who receives something inaccurate will hold your company responsible, not the tool.

FundPilot's assistant is built this way: it answers from live CRM data and drafts applications, emails and texts, but it never sends or saves anything without a person confirming it.

Let the assistant hold the pen. Keep the signature and the send button with a person.

Leg 04Other things an assistant should stay away from

Beyond sending, there are judgment calls that belong with experienced people. An assistant can surface information that helps those calls, but should not make them.

  • Promising a merchant an approval, a rate or a funding timeline
  • Deciding whether a merchant can responsibly take on another position
  • Interpreting legal or regulatory requirements for your shop
  • Negotiating with a merchant or a lender on your behalf
  • Inventing figures when the data is missing instead of saying so

Leg 05Where your data goes matters

Every question you ask an assistant about a merchant involves that merchant's information: bank activity, revenue, sometimes personal details of the owners. Before adopting any AI tool, ask where that data is processed, whether it is used for anything beyond answering you, and who can see the history of what was asked.

Some shops are comfortable with a well-secured hosted service. Others, particularly those handling large volumes of sensitive files, prefer a private AI option running on private hardware so merchant data stays within a tighter boundary. Either way, the assistant should respect the same role-based access as the rest of the CRM, so a broker cannot ask it about deals they are not permitted to see. Confirm your obligations with qualified counsel.

Leg 06How to test an AI assistant in a demo

Demos are designed to look good. Bring your own scenarios and watch what happens at the edges.

  1. Ask about a deal and then check the answer against the underlying record.
  2. Ask a question the data cannot answer and see whether it admits it rather than guessing.
  3. Request a merchant text and confirm nothing goes out until you approve it.
  4. Log in as a broker and ask about a deal assigned to someone else.
  5. Ask where prompts and merchant data are processed and stored.
  6. Have a newer broker try it, since they will rely on it most.

Questions from the floor

Will an AI assistant replace junior brokers?

That is not what a well-designed assistant is for. It removes repetitive writing and lookup work, which tends to help newer brokers most because they spend longer on those tasks. The conversations, the relationship with the merchant and the judgment about what is responsible to offer still need a person, and that is where you want junior brokers spending their growing skill.

Can the assistant listen to calls?

In FundPilot, call recordings become searchable transcripts with summaries, and the assistant can draw on those when answering questions or drafting follow-ups. Recording calls carries consent obligations that vary by jurisdiction, so confirm your requirements with qualified counsel and make sure your disclosures are in place before you rely on recordings.

What if the assistant gets something wrong?

It will, occasionally, which is exactly why human confirmation matters. Treat every draft as a first pass from a quick but new assistant: read it, check figures against the file, and fix tone. Over time you learn where it is reliable and where to look closely. If it ever answers with confidence about data that does not exist, raise it with your vendor.
