Onboarding a New Broker: The First 30 Days

A four-week ramp plan that moves a new hire from listening in on calls to submitting clean files, without throwing live merchants at them on day one.

By the FundPilot teamUpdated October 15, 2026

  3. Week two: practice before live dials
  4. Week three: owning a small pipeline

  6. Milestones that actually show progress
  7. Questions

The usual onboarding at a funding shop goes like this: a desk, a headset, a login, a stack of leads and a seasoned broker who says to listen to a few calls and then start dialing. By the end of week two the new hire has burned through a lead list, talked a few merchants into applications that were never fundable, and started to wonder whether this job was a mistake.

It does not have to work that way. A new broker needs to learn three different things at once: the product, the conversation and the process. Teaching them in a deliberate order, with practice before live dials and clear milestones each week, shortens the ramp and protects your lead spend and your lender relationships. Here is a thirty-day plan you can adapt to your own floor.

Pre-flight summary

  • Fix logins, permissions and dialer setup before day one.
  • Teach product, conversation and process in that order.
  • Use a practice environment before live dials, then low-risk lists.
  • Review every early file before it reaches a lender.
  • Judge the first month on milestones and file quality, not fundings.

Leg 01Before day one: set up the seat properly

Half of a bad first week is friction that has nothing to do with selling. Logins that do not work, a dialer that has not been assigned a number, permissions that hide the deals they are supposed to study. Fix all of it before the person walks in.

Give them a named mentor, not just a desk next to one. The mentor should know they are responsible for this person for a month and should have a few hours a week freed up to do it.

  • CRM account with a role that fits a trainee, with two-factor sign-in turned on
  • Dialer access in a practice environment, plus a caller ID ready for live work later
  • A reading list: your product sheet, lender guideline summaries, a glossary of floor terms
  • Five funded deal files to study, with the call recordings that led to them
  • A calendar with shadowing blocks already booked

Leg 02Week one: listen, read and learn the vocabulary

The first week is about understanding what you sell and to whom. A new broker should be able to explain, in plain words, that a merchant cash advance is generally a purchase of future receivables rather than a loan, what a factor rate is, how a daily or weekly remittance works, and why existing positions matter to a funder.

Pair that reading with listening. Have the trainee sit in on live calls with two or three different brokers, then review recordings and transcripts of calls that went well and calls that did not. Ask them to write down every question the merchant asked. By Friday they should have a list of the twenty questions merchants raise most, and the honest answer to each.

Make one session about how statements are read. Even if underwriting sits with someone else, a broker who understands deposits, balances and NSFs asks better qualification questions on the first call.

Leg 03Week two: practice before live dials

Week two is rehearsal. Role-play is useful but limited, because colleagues pretending to be merchants are rarely as busy, suspicious or distracted as the real thing. A practice environment that looks exactly like the live system lets the trainee build muscle memory without risking a real lead.

FundPilot ships with a safe practice mode for new brokers: the same screens, dialer flow and deal records, with nothing reaching a real merchant or lender. A trainee can create a deal, log a call, request statements through the merchant portal flow and move a file through your stages until the clicks are automatic.

End the week with live calls on a small, low-risk list, such as aged leads, with the mentor listening in. Keep it to an hour or two a day. The goal is comfort, not volume.

Practise the clicks until they disappear, so the trainee's attention is on the merchant and not on the screen.

Leg 04Week three: owning a small pipeline

Now the trainee gets their own lead allocation, sized well below a full broker's. They dial, qualify, collect applications and statements, and build files. The mentor reviews every file before it goes to a lender.

Say the trainee brings in an application from a restaurant showing healthy deposits, but the statements reveal two existing daily debits to other funders. Week three is where they learn to spot that before submission, explain it honestly to the merchant, and route the file to lenders whose guidelines allow for it rather than sending it everywhere.

Review two or three of their recorded calls each day together. Look for one thing to keep and one thing to change. More than that is noise.

Leg 05Week four: first submissions and the handoff to normal

In week four the trainee submits files under supervision and, if your structure allows, presents an offer with the mentor on the line. The aim is not a funded deal by day thirty; funding depends on too many factors outside their control. The aim is a broker who can run the whole process cleanly.

At the end of the month, hold a short review with the trainee, the mentor and the manager. Agree what the next sixty days look like: lead allocation, coaching cadence and which milestones come next. If you run FundPilot, this is also the moment to move the trainee's role from practice to a live broker profile, so their access widens on purpose rather than by accident.

Leg 06Milestones that actually show progress

Measure activities and quality during the ramp, not revenue. A trainee who hits these milestones is on track even if nothing has funded yet.

  1. Day 5: explains the product, factor rate and remittance in plain words, unprompted
  2. Day 10: completes a full practice deal from first call to submission without help
  3. Day 15: holds live calls with a contact-to-application rate the mentor is comfortable with
  4. Day 20: submits a file the mentor approves with no corrections
  5. Day 25: handles the five common merchant objections honestly on recorded calls
  6. Day 30: manages a small live pipeline with no deals past their stage limit

Questions from the floor

How long does it take a new broker to fund their first deal?

It varies widely and depends on lead quality, the merchant mix, your lenders and the broker's background, so avoid promising a timeline to the hire or to yourself. Focus the first month on running the process cleanly. A broker who builds complete, honest files and handles conversations well tends to start funding as their pipeline matures, while one rushed into volume often produces declines.

Should new brokers start on fresh leads or aged leads?

Start with aged or lower-cost leads while they find their footing. Fresh leads are expensive and time-sensitive, and a nervous first call can waste one. Once the trainee is comfortable and the mentor has heard enough recordings to trust their qualification questions, phase in fresh leads gradually. Keep allocation small until file quality is consistently good.

What does a mentor actually need to do?

Book shadowing time, review recordings with the trainee a few times a week, check every file before submission during the first month, and be available for quick questions. The mentor does not need to teach everything; the reading list and practice mode handle much of it. Recognise the mentor's time in their own targets so the role is not resented.

Do new brokers need to be licensed?

Licensing and registration requirements for commercial finance brokers differ by state and are changing in several places, so there is no single answer. Before a new hire speaks with merchants about offers, confirm with qualified counsel which requirements apply to your shop and that role, and build any registration steps into the onboarding plan.
