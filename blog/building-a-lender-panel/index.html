Building a Lender Panel That Covers Every Kind of File

A panel is not a list of every funder who will take your call. It is a map of who funds what, kept current, so no reasonable file walks out the door unplaced.

By the FundPilot teamUpdated October 15, 2026

Route

  2. Tier funders by appetite, not by how much you like them
  3. Track guidelines as data, not as PDFs in a folder
  4. Decide how many funders is enough
  5. Keep relationships warm between deals
  6. Use outcomes to prune and grow the panel
  7. Questions

It is Thursday afternoon and a broker on your floor is holding a file nobody wants. A landscaping company, eleven months in business, strong deposits from April to October and thin ones the rest of the year. The two funders your shop sends almost everything to have already passed. The broker is scrolling an old email thread, trying to remember the name of the rep who once mentioned they liked seasonal trades.

That moment is a panel problem, not a sales problem. When your shop's lender relationships live in a few people's heads and inboxes, every unusual file becomes a scavenger hunt. A deliberate lender panel turns that hunt into a lookup: you know which funders cover which profiles, what they currently want, and who to call when the file sits outside the lines.

Pre-flight summary

  • Build the panel from the file profiles your floor actually sees, starting with the gaps.
  • Tier funders by risk appetite and send each file to the lowest-cost tier it honestly fits.
  • Store guidelines as dated, searchable fields so matching is a lookup, not a memory test.
  • Give every funder relationship a named owner and log what they learn.
  • Let submission and funding outcomes tell you which relationships to grow or retire.

Leg 01Start from the files you actually see

Before you add a single funder, pull the last few months of submissions and sort them by profile. Most floors find their volume clusters into a handful of shapes: established businesses with clean statements, mid-tier files with a position or two already open, younger businesses, seasonal trades, and a long tail of restricted or unusual industries.

Each cluster needs coverage, and the clusters you see most need depth, meaning more than one funder able to take the file. If eighty files a month look like second-position restaurants and you have exactly one funder who does second positions on restaurants, you do not have a panel for that profile. You have a single point of failure.

  • Count files by time in business, monthly deposit range, position count and industry.
  • Mark which clusters currently have zero, one, or several realistic funders.
  • Note the files that went unplaced and why, even roughly.
  • Treat that gap list as your recruiting brief for new relationships.

Leg 02Tier funders by appetite, not by how much you like them

A useful way to organize a panel is by the kind of risk each funder is built to take. Your first tier covers the strongest files: longer time in business, steady deposits, few or no open positions. A second tier takes files with more history on them, such as an existing advance or some negative days. A third tier handles the younger, thinner or more complicated files, usually at a higher cost to the merchant.

Tiering matters for the merchant as much as for you. A merchant who qualifies for first-tier terms should be shown first-tier options before anything else. Sending a strong file straight to a funder built for difficult credit costs the merchant money and tells them, eventually, that you were not working for them.

Alongside tiers, keep a niche column. Some funders specialize in trucking, medical practices, construction or seasonal businesses. They may sit in any tier, but when a file matches their niche they are often the first call.

Tier funders by the risk they are built to take, then send every file to the lowest-cost tier it honestly fits.

Leg 03Track guidelines as data, not as PDFs in a folder

Every funder publishes or tells you their guidelines: minimum time in business, minimum monthly revenue, maximum positions, excluded industries, excluded states, minimum credit if they check it, and the documents they require. The trouble is that guidelines change, and a PDF saved last spring does not update itself.

Store guidelines as structured fields per funder, with a date on each one showing when it was last confirmed. That lets you filter: show me every funder who takes 6 months in business, under $30,000 in monthly deposits, with one open position. In FundPilot, lender guidelines live on each lender record, and lender matching scores a file against them before submission, so brokers see the realistic options instead of guessing.

Add a free-text notes field too. Some of the most valuable panel knowledge is soft: this funder will stretch on time in business if deposits are strong, this one dislikes heavy cash businesses, this rep wants a call before a large file comes in.

Leg 04Decide how many funders is enough

More funders is not automatically better. Every relationship needs onboarding, an agreement, a contact who answers, and periodic attention. A panel of eighty funders that your team only uses twelve of is mostly noise, and it tempts brokers into sending files everywhere.

A practical target is depth where your volume is and breadth where your gaps are. For your heaviest clusters, aim for a few funders per tier so you can compare offers honestly. For rare profiles, one or two dependable niche relationships may be enough.

Review usage quarterly. If a funder has received no submissions in months, either they no longer fit your book or nobody remembers them. Both are worth knowing.

Leg 05Keep relationships warm between deals

Guidelines tell you what a funder will do on paper. Relationships tell you what they will do on a borderline file at four o'clock on a Friday. That second kind of knowledge only exists if someone on your side maintains it.

Assign an owner to each funder relationship, usually a senior broker or manager. That person checks in on guideline changes, flags when approvals from that funder slow down, and is the escalation point when a file stalls. Log those conversations on the lender record so the knowledge survives if the owner leaves.

  1. Assign one relationship owner per funder and record it on the lender profile.
  2. Confirm guidelines with each active funder on a set schedule and update the confirmed date.
  3. Review submission-to-approval and approval-to-funded outcomes per funder each month.
  4. Raise problems early and directly: slow responses, changed appetite, recurring stip requests.
  5. Share wins too, such as a renewal on a merchant they funded, so the relationship is not only complaints.

Leg 06Use outcomes to prune and grow the panel

Once submissions, replies and offers are tracked per funder, the panel starts managing itself. You can see which funders approve the profiles they say they want, which ones decline files that fit their stated guidelines, and which approvals actually fund. FundPilot threads lender replies onto the deal and lays offers side by side, so that history builds up as a by-product of normal work rather than a separate reporting chore.

Use that history carefully. A funder that declines a lot may simply be stricter than its guideline sheet suggests, which is useful to know rather than a reason to drop them. A funder that approves and then changes terms at contract is a different matter, and worth a direct conversation with the relationship owner.

When a recurring file profile keeps going unplaced, that is your signal to recruit. Go looking specifically for a funder who covers that gap, rather than adding whoever happens to email you this week.

Questions from the floor

How many funders should a small brokerage have on its panel?

There is no right number, but a small shop is usually better served by a focused group it actually uses than a long list it does not. Aim for at least two or three options in each tier where you have regular volume, so you can compare offers for the merchant, plus a few niche relationships that cover the profiles you see occasionally. Add funders to close specific gaps rather than to make the list longer.

How often should lender guidelines be reconfirmed?

Often enough that brokers trust what the record says. Many shops reconfirm active funders monthly or quarterly and immediately after any sign of change, such as a run of unexpected declines. Put the date of the last confirmation on each guideline record so a broker can see at a glance whether the information is fresh or should be checked with the relationship owner before submitting.

Should brokers be allowed to add funders to the panel themselves?

It usually works better when new funders go through a manager. Onboarding a funder involves an agreement, commission terms and a check that they treat merchants fairly. A broker can propose a new relationship and explain the gap it fills, then a manager reviews and adds it. That keeps the panel coherent and avoids files going to funders nobody has vetted.
