Call Scripts That Don't Sound Scripted

Merchants hang up on a recital. Build a framework script with fixed checkpoints and natural language in between, and your newest broker will sound like a person.

By the FundPilot teamUpdated October 15, 2026

Business owners can hear a script within about four words. Hi, this is Mike, how are you doing today? The merchant is standing in a kitchen, or on a job site, or behind a register with a line of customers, and the rhythm of a read-aloud opener tells them everything they need to know about the next ninety seconds. They are already reaching for the end-call button.

And yet a floor without scripts has its own problems. New brokers ramble, skip qualification questions, and make up answers to objections. Experienced brokers drift into habits that nobody has reviewed in years. The answer is not to choose between scripted and unscripted. It is to write a different kind of script: one that fixes what must happen on the call and leaves how it is said to the person saying it.

Pre-flight summary

  • Use word-for-word wording only where precision is required, such as disclosures.
  • Build first calls around five checkpoints with sample phrasings.
  • Keep a bank of open discovery questions brokers can choose from.
  • Turn rebuttals into branches: acknowledge, ask, answer honestly.
  • Judge scripts by outcomes and recordings, and update them monthly.

Leg 01Word-for-word versus framework

A word-for-word script specifies every sentence. It is easy to write and easy to audit, and it is useful for a handful of moments where precise wording matters, such as required disclosures or a recording notice. For the rest of the call it produces stiff delivery and breaks the moment the merchant says something unexpected.

A framework script specifies checkpoints: the outcome each part of the call must achieve, the information that must be gathered, and a few example phrasings. The broker chooses their own words to get there. It takes a little longer to train, but it survives contact with real merchants, and it sounds like a conversation because it is one.

Leg 02The five checkpoints of a first call

Here is a framework for a first conversation with a merchant who submitted an inquiry. Each checkpoint has a goal; the example lines are starting points, not lines to memorise.

  1. Permission: confirm you have the right person and that now is a workable time. Example: I saw your request come through this morning. Do you have five minutes, or is there a better time today?
  2. Reason: find out why they are looking. Example: What's going on in the business that has you thinking about funding?
  3. Shape: gather time in business, approximate monthly deposits, industry and any existing funding.
  4. Fit: explain honestly what kind of funding might suit them, how it works and what it costs in plain terms.
  5. Next step: agree on one concrete action, such as sending the application and three months of statements today, and confirm how they will send them.

Leg 03Discovery questions that do the heavy lifting

Most of a good call is the merchant talking. Open questions get you there, and they also give you the information a lender will ask about anyway. Write a bank of them into the framework and let brokers pick what fits.

Notice that several of these questions surface things a merchant might not volunteer, like an existing position or a recent slow month. Asking openly and early is fairer to the merchant and saves everyone from surprises at underwriting.

  • What would you use the funds for, and what does that do for the business?
  • How have the last few months compared with the same time last year?
  • Do you have any existing advances or business loans being repaid right now?
  • Is your revenue steady through the year, or are there busy and slow seasons?
  • How quickly do you need this, and what happens if it takes longer?
  • Who else is involved in the decision?

Write the outcome of each checkpoint, not the sentence. Let the broker find the words.

Leg 04Objection branches, not rebuttals

Old-style scripts include a rebuttal for every objection, delivered the instant the merchant raises it. Merchants recognise that pattern and push back harder. A branch works differently: acknowledge, ask a question to understand the real concern, then answer honestly.

Say the merchant says the daily payment sounds scary. A rebuttal says everybody worries about that but it is really very manageable. A branch says that is a fair concern; can I ask what your average daily deposits look like, so we can see whether a daily or weekly schedule would actually fit? The second version treats the merchant as a business owner and gathers information you need anyway.

Sometimes the honest answer to an objection is that this product is not a good fit. Build that branch into the script too. A merchant you decline politely today may come back when the timing is right, and will remember how they were treated.

Leg 05Testing whether a script works

Scripts should be judged by outcomes and by listening, not by how they read on paper. Compare first-call-to-application rates before and after a change, and listen to a sample of calls to hear how brokers are actually using the framework.

FundPilot turns call recordings into searchable transcripts with summaries, so you can search across a week of calls for a phrase like existing position and hear how different brokers handle that moment. That is far quicker than listening to whole calls at random, and it shows you which phrasings land.

Leg 06Keeping the script alive

A script written once and filed away decays. Brokers improvise, some improvisations are better than the original, and nobody captures them. Make the framework a living document: store it inside the CRM where brokers can open it during a call, review it monthly, and add the best real phrasings you hear on recordings.

Questions from the floor

Should new brokers start with a word-for-word script?

A short word-for-word opener can calm nerves on the first few days, but move them to the framework quickly. If a new broker only knows the exact words, they freeze when the merchant goes off script, which happens on almost every call. Practise the checkpoints in role-play and practice mode until the broker can reach each outcome in their own words.

How long should a first call last?

Long enough to reach all five checkpoints and no longer. Some merchants answer everything in a few minutes; others want to talk through their business at length, and that is fine if the conversation is productive. What matters is that the call ends with a concrete next step. A long call with no agreed action is less valuable than a short one with a clear commitment.

Do scripts need to include a recording notice or disclosures?

If you record calls or are subject to disclosure requirements, the wording and timing of notices can matter, and requirements differ by jurisdiction and change over time. Treat those sections as fixed, word-for-word text approved by qualified counsel. Keep them separate from the conversational framework so brokers never paraphrase them by accident.

What if a broker's own approach outperforms the script?

Study it and fold the useful parts into the framework. Listen to their recordings, identify which phrasings or question orders make the difference, and test them with a couple of other brokers before rolling them out. The best scripts are usually built from what the strongest people on the floor already do, written down so everyone can learn it.
