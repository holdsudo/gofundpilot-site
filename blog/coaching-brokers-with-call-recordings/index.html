Coaching Brokers With Call Recordings and Transcripts

Recordings only improve a floor if someone listens with a purpose. A review cadence, a scorecard brokers respect, and the notice questions to settle first.

Most funding shops record calls, and most of those recordings are never heard. They pile up by the thousand, consulted only when a merchant disputes what was said or a deal goes wrong. Meanwhile the manager coaches from memory and impressions: she seems a bit rushed on the phone, he does not ask enough questions. Brokers hear vague feedback, nod, and change nothing.

A recording is the most honest coaching tool a floor has. It shows exactly what was said, in what order, and how the merchant reacted. Turned into a searchable transcript with a summary, it becomes something a busy manager can actually use. What turns that raw material into better brokers is a system: what to listen to, how often, what to score, and how to deliver the feedback so it sticks.

Pre-flight summary

  • Confirm recording notice and consent practices with counsel before building on them.
  • Review two or three targeted calls per broker each week.
  • Choose calls that answer a specific question about performance.
  • Use a short scorecard and avoid comparative totals.
  • Give one keep and one change, then listen for it next week.

Leg 01Settle notice and consent first

Before building a coaching programme on recordings, make sure your recording practice itself is sound. Rules on notice and consent for recording calls differ between jurisdictions, and when a broker in one state calls a merchant in another, more than one set of rules may be relevant. Some shops play an automated notice; others have brokers state it at the start of every call.

This is an area to confirm with qualified counsel rather than settle by habit, and to revisit periodically, since requirements change. Once you have an approved approach, build the notice into your call framework as fixed wording and check for it during reviews. This article describes coaching practices, not legal requirements.

Leg 02A review cadence that survives busy weeks

Coaching programmes rarely fail because the manager disagrees with them. They fail because a busy week arrives and reviews get skipped, then skipped again. Make the cadence small enough to survive the worst week of the month.

A workable rhythm for most floors is two or three calls per broker per week, chosen deliberately, reviewed in a short one-to-one. Add a monthly group session where the team hears one excellent call together.

  • Weekly: two or three calls per broker, scored and discussed in fifteen minutes
  • Weekly: one call of the week shared with the whole floor
  • Monthly: a group session on a single skill, such as presenting offers
  • Quarterly: a review of the scorecard itself, adjusted to what you are learning
  • As needed: immediate review of any call involving a complaint or dispute

Leg 03Choosing which calls to review

Random selection sounds fair but mostly produces voicemails and two-minute wrong numbers. Choose calls tied to a specific question about the broker's performance.

Say a broker's applications often stall before documents arrive. Pull their last few calls where an application was signed and listen to how they asked for statements. Did they agree a specific time and method? Did they explain why the documents were needed? The problem usually becomes obvious within three calls.

Searchable transcripts make this targeting fast. In FundPilot, call recordings are turned into transcripts with summaries, so a manager can search one broker's week for words like statements or existing position and jump straight to the relevant moment rather than listening from the beginning.

Choose calls that answer a question about the broker. Random calls mostly teach you about voicemail.

Leg 04A scorecard brokers will respect

A scorecard keeps reviews consistent and stops feedback depending on the manager's mood. Keep it short. Long scorecards turn coaching into an audit and brokers start performing for the form rather than the merchant.

Score each item simply: done well, done, missed. Avoid numerical totals that invite comparison between brokers. The goal is a conversation about one or two specific behaviours, not a grade.

  1. Opening: confirmed the right person and a workable time, gave any required notice
  2. Discovery: asked open questions and let the merchant explain their situation
  3. Qualification: gathered time in business, deposits, industry and existing positions
  4. Honesty: explained cost, remittance and fit accurately, with no loan language for advances
  5. Objections: acknowledged and asked before answering
  6. Next step: ended with a specific, agreed action and timing

Leg 05Delivering feedback that changes behaviour

The review conversation matters more than the scoring. Start by asking the broker how they think the call went; they often spot the issue themselves, and self-identified problems get fixed faster. Then play the specific moment rather than describing it.

Pick one thing to keep and one thing to change. Not five. A broker who leaves a review with one clear adjustment, such as agreeing a statement deadline before ending every call, will try it that afternoon. A broker who leaves with a list will try nothing.

Follow up the next week by listening for that exact behaviour. When it improves, say so. Recognition of a specific change is far more motivating than general praise.

Leg 06Building a library of great calls

Over time, your best recordings become a training asset. Tag strong examples by situation: a clean first call, a well-handled rate objection, an honest decline, an offer explained clearly. New brokers can work through the library during onboarding, and experienced ones can find an example when they face a tricky conversation.

Keep access sensible. Recordings contain merchant information, so the library should live inside your CRM with role-based access rather than as audio files passed around. FundPilot keeps an audit trail of downloads and exports, which helps you see if recordings leave the system. Questions from the floor

How much time does call coaching take for a manager?

With transcripts and summaries, reviewing two or three targeted calls per broker can take around fifteen to twenty minutes per person per week, including the conversation. For a floor of eight brokers that is a few hours a week. The time pays for itself if it prevents even a small number of lost deals or weak submissions, and it is far more effective than general feedback.

Should brokers review their own calls?

Yes, and many improve faster that way. Ask each broker to choose one of their own calls each week, score it against the same scorecard, and bring it to their one-to-one. Self-review builds the habit of listening critically, and brokers are often harder on themselves than a manager would be. The manager's role becomes guiding and confirming rather than correcting.

Can recordings be used for discipline?

They can document serious problems such as misrepresentation or pressure tactics, and those should be addressed firmly. But if recordings are mainly used to catch people out, brokers become guarded and coaching suffers. Keep the weekly programme focused on improvement, handle compliance concerns through a separate clear process, and follow your employment policies and legal advice.

Are AI call summaries accurate enough to coach from?

Summaries are a good way to find the right calls and moments quickly, but coach from the recording and transcript themselves. A summary may compress or miss tone and nuance. Use it to decide which call to open and where to jump to, then listen to the actual exchange before giving feedback. That keeps the conversation grounded in what was really said.
