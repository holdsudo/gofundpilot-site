Commercial Financing Disclosure Laws: A Plain-English Overview

Several states now expect business-funding offers to come with standardized disclosures. Here is what the concept means for your floor, and what to ask your lawyer.

A merchant calls back on a Thursday afternoon with an offer in hand and one question: what is this actually going to cost me? For years the answer depended on who picked up the phone. Some brokers walked through the purchase price, the holdback and the estimated term line by line. Others read the payment amount and moved on to the signature page.

A growing number of states have decided that answer should not depend on the broker. Commercial financing disclosure laws, in their various forms, ask providers and sometimes brokers to present the cost and structure of a business-funding offer in a standardized way before the merchant signs. If your shop funds or brokers deals across state lines, these laws are no longer a niche topic for the legal department. They shape how offers leave your floor.

This guide explains the general concepts in plain English. It is not legal advice, and it deliberately does not state any particular state's current rules, thresholds or deadlines as fact. Those details vary, change and get interpreted differently, so treat everything here as a framework for a conversation with qualified counsel.

Pre-flight summary

  • Commercial financing disclosure laws aim to give merchants standardized cost information before they sign.
  • Coverage, thresholds and responsibilities differ by state and change over time; confirm specifics with qualified counsel.
  • Brokers should present offers from the disclosure itself, never from a conflicting verbal summary.
  • Store disclosures on the deal and enforce their order in your pipeline so the process survives busy months.

Leg 01What these laws are trying to fix

Consumer lending has long had standardized cost disclosures. Commercial funding, including merchant cash advances, historically did not, partly because an MCA is generally structured as a purchase of future receivables rather than a loan. That structure makes an apples-to-apples comparison harder for a merchant holding two offers with different factor rates, holdback percentages and fee schedules.

Disclosure laws generally aim to close that gap. The common thread is simple: before a merchant commits, they should see the key economic terms in a consistent format, so they can compare offers and understand what they are agreeing to. How each state defines those terms, which transactions are covered and who is responsible for delivering the disclosure are where the details diverge.

Leg 02The concepts that tend to show up

You will not find one uniform statute, but certain building blocks recur across the regimes that exist. Knowing the vocabulary makes your conversation with counsel faster and cheaper.

  • Total amount of funds provided, and how much the merchant actually receives after fees are deducted.
  • Total repayment or purchased amount, meaning the full dollar figure the merchant will remit over the life of the deal.
  • Some form of estimated cost metric, which in certain regimes is expressed as an annualized rate and in others is presented differently.
  • Payment mechanics: frequency, amount or holdback percentage, and how estimates were calculated for products without a fixed term.
  • Prepayment terms, including whether any discount applies if the merchant pays early.
  • Coverage thresholds and exemptions, which commonly turn on deal size, provider type or transaction type.
  • Who must deliver the disclosure and when, and whether a signed acknowledgment is expected.

Leg 03Where brokers fit in

A common question on the floor is whether disclosure is the funder's problem or the broker's. The honest answer is that it depends on the law in question and on how your agreements with funders allocate responsibility. Some frameworks place obligations primarily on the provider of the financing. Some reach brokers directly, for example through registration requirements or rules about how brokers describe offers and compensation.

Even where the formal obligation sits with the funder, the broker is usually the person physically presenting the offer. If a broker paraphrases the terms on a call in a way that contradicts the written disclosure, that is a problem for everyone involved, including the merchant. Your counsel can tell you exactly where your shop's obligations begin and end. Your sales process should make sure brokers never undercut the document the merchant is supposed to rely on.

Even when the funder owns the disclosure, the broker owns the conversation. The two should never tell different stories.

Leg 04How disclosure rules change the workflow on your floor

Consider a hypothetical. A broker has a restaurant owner with two offers: one with a lower factor rate but a daily remittance, another with a slightly higher factor rate and weekly payments. Under a disclosure regime, each offer may arrive with its own standardized summary. The broker's job shifts from selling one number to walking the merchant through the documents side by side.

That shift has practical consequences. Offers need to be stored with their disclosures attached, not floating in an inbox. The version the merchant signed needs to be retrievable months later if a question comes up. And the order of events matters: if a law expects disclosure before signing, your process should make it hard to send a contract without the disclosure having gone out first.

Leg 05Building a compliance routine you can actually follow

Laws in this area keep evolving, and a binder of last year's guidance is not a compliance program. A practical routine looks more like a recurring process than a one-time project.

FundPilot's custom pipeline stages make that kind of rule straightforward to express, so a deal cannot quietly skip the disclosure step on a busy afternoon.

  1. Map where your merchants and funders are located, and list every state you regularly touch.
  2. Bring that map to qualified counsel and ask which regimes apply to your shop specifically, as a broker, a funder or both.
  3. Get written guidance on who prepares each disclosure, who delivers it, and what proof of delivery you should keep.
  4. Turn that guidance into pipeline rules, for example a required document before a deal can move to the contracts stage.
  5. Train brokers to present offers using the disclosure itself, not a verbal summary of it.
  6. Schedule a periodic review with counsel, because thresholds, effective dates and interpretations change.

Leg 06Common mistakes that create avoidable risk

The most frequent problem is not bad intent. It is drift. A shop sets up a clean process, then a busy month arrives and brokers start sending contracts from personal email with the disclosure attached separately, or not at all. Another frequent problem is assuming a deal is exempt because it was exempt last time, when the merchant's location, the deal size or the product changed.

A third mistake is letting sales language contradict the paperwork. Phrases like guaranteed approval, no cost or lowest rate available can create trouble regardless of what the disclosure says. Keep brokers describing offers in neutral, accurate terms and let the merchant compare the documents. If you are unsure whether a phrase is acceptable, that is a question for counsel, not for the sales manager.

Questions from the floor

Do disclosure laws apply to merchant cash advances if an MCA is not a loan?

Some disclosure regimes are written to cover a range of commercial financing products, which can include transactions structured as purchases of future receivables. Whether a particular law covers a particular product depends on its definitions, exemptions and thresholds. Do not assume an MCA falls outside a law because it is not a loan. Ask qualified counsel to review your specific products and the states where your merchants are located.

As a broker, am I responsible for delivering disclosures?

It depends on the law and on your agreements with funders. Some frameworks place the obligation mainly on the provider; others impose duties on brokers, such as registration or rules about how offers and compensation are described. Get written guidance from counsel on your role, then build your process so brokers always present the official disclosure rather than their own summary of the terms.

How often should we review our disclosure process?

Treat it as recurring work rather than a one-time setup. New laws take effect, regulators issue guidance and interpretations shift. Many shops set a regular review with counsel and revisit the process whenever they expand into new states, add a new product or onboard a new funder. The right cadence for your shop is something to agree with your legal advisor.

Can software make us compliant?

No software makes a shop compliant on its own. A CRM can help you follow the process your counsel designs: keeping disclosures attached to deals, requiring documents before a stage change and logging when files were downloaded or changed. The legal judgment about which rules apply and what the disclosure must contain still has to come from qualified professionals.
