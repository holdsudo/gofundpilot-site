Factor Rates vs. APR: Explaining Cost Clearly

A factor rate tells a merchant how much they pay back. It does not tell them how fast. Here is how to explain both without hiding the ball or overcomplicating it.

A merchant hears 1.25 and thinks 25 percent. It is an understandable leap. Most business owners have a car payment, a mortgage or a credit card, and every one of those is quoted as a yearly rate. So when a funding offer comes with a factor rate, they map it onto the only cost language they know.

That mapping is where misunderstandings begin. A factor rate and an annual percentage rate measure different things, and the gap between them grows the faster the money is paid back. Brokers who can explain that difference simply, with honest numbers, earn trust that lasts well past the first deal. Brokers who blur it tend to hear about it later, usually in the form of an unhappy merchant.

Pre-flight summary

  • A factor rate tells you total payback; it says nothing about how fast it is paid.
  • The same factor rate means a much higher annualized cost when paid back quickly.
  • Always start cost conversations from the net amount the merchant actually receives.
  • Any annualized figure for an MCA is an estimate built on assumptions about payment pace.
  • Clear, consistent cost language protects merchants and the shop alike.

Leg 01What each number actually measures

A factor rate is a multiplier. Multiply the funding amount by it and you get the total the merchant will pay back. On $30,000 at a 1.25 factor rate, the merchant pays back $37,500, so the cost of the funding is $7,500. Time does not appear anywhere in that calculation.

An APR expresses the cost of credit as a yearly rate, taking into account how long the money is outstanding and how the balance shrinks as payments are made. It was built for loans with defined terms.

That distinction matters for merchant cash advances in particular. An MCA is generally structured as a purchase of future receivables, not a loan, and the time it takes to collect depends on the merchant's sales. So any APR-style figure for an advance rests on an assumption about how quickly payments will be made. It is an estimate, and it should be presented as one.

Leg 02Why time changes everything

Take the same hypothetical $30,000 at a 1.25 factor, with the $7,500 cost. Imagine two versions of it. These figures are illustrations only, not quotes or typical terms.

In the first, payments are made every business day and the full $37,500 is delivered in about six months. In the second, payments are smaller and the full amount is delivered in about twelve months. The dollar cost is identical: $7,500 in both cases.

But in the six-month version, the merchant has use of a declining balance for half as long. On average they hold roughly half the original amount over that period, about $15,000. Paying $7,500 for the use of around $15,000 for half a year works out to an annualized cost in the rough neighbourhood of 100 percent. Stretch the same payback over twelve months and the rough annualized figure falls to about half that.

Neither number is a formal APR calculation; a real one depends on the exact payment schedule, fees and the method used. The point of the illustration is the direction: the same factor rate produces a much higher annualized cost when paid back quickly.

Same factor rate, same dollar cost, very different annualized figure. Speed of payback is the hidden variable.

Leg 03Fees move both numbers

If an origination fee is deducted at funding, the merchant receives less than the approved amount but still pays back the full total. In the example, a $1,500 fee means the merchant receives $28,500 and pays back $37,500, so the cost is $9,000, not $7,500. Any honest comparison, factor-based or annualized, has to start from the cash that actually reaches the merchant's account.

This is the single most common place where cost gets understated in conversation. A broker quotes the factor rate on the gross amount and never mentions that the net amount is smaller. The merchant discovers the difference when the deposit lands.

When you lay several offers side by side in FundPilot's offer comparison, put the net amount next to the total payback for each one, so the merchant sees cost from the cash they actually receive.

Leg 04How to explain it on the phone

You do not need a spreadsheet on the call. You need three plain statements and the merchant's agreement that they follow them. Start with dollars, because dollars are what the merchant will actually feel. Then add time, because time is what the factor rate leaves out. Then compare, because the merchant may have other options.

  1. State the net amount: here is what lands in your account after fees.
  2. State the total payback and the dollar cost: you pay back this much, so the funding costs you this much.
  3. State the payment and the estimated pace: this amount, this often, which at your current sales takes about this long.
  4. Explain that a faster payback means a higher cost on a yearly basis, even though the dollar figure stays the same.
  5. If an estimated annualized figure has been provided in a disclosure, walk through it and what it assumes.
  6. Ask what questions they have and answer them before moving on.

Leg 05Disclosures and why they exist

Some states have passed commercial financing disclosure laws that require providers to give businesses specific cost information before they sign, and some of those call for an estimated annualized rate alongside other figures. Which transactions are covered, who is responsible for providing the disclosure and the exact method all vary and continue to change. Confirm current requirements with qualified counsel; this is general information, not legal advice.

The purpose behind these rules is straightforward: business owners were comparing products quoted in incompatible terms. Brokers who already explain cost clearly tend to find disclosures easy to work with, because the disclosure simply confirms what the merchant has already heard.

Leg 06Honest framing that still lets you do your job

None of this means talking merchants out of funding. For many businesses, fast access to capital is worth a high annualized cost: covering a payroll gap, buying inventory at a discount, or taking a job that pays well. The cost is real, and so is the value. Your role is to make sure the merchant weighs both with accurate information.

Avoid phrases that blur the product, such as calling an advance a loan, describing the factor rate as an interest rate, or calling a cost low without context. Avoid the reverse too: telling a merchant APR does not apply to them in a way that suggests cost is not worth thinking about.

Questions from the floor

Can you convert a factor rate directly into an APR?

Not with the factor rate alone. An annualized figure depends on how long payback takes, how often payments are made, and what fees are deducted. For a merchant cash advance, the payback period depends on sales, so any annualized number rests on an assumed pace of payments. You can estimate it, and some disclosures do, but it should always be presented as an estimate with its assumptions explained.

Is it wrong to call a factor rate an interest rate?

It is misleading. Interest is a charge for borrowed money over time, while a factor rate is a fixed multiplier on the funding amount regardless of time. For merchant cash advances there is a further issue: the product is generally structured as a purchase of future receivables, not a loan. Using loan language can confuse the merchant about what they are agreeing to.

Why do merchants still choose funding with a high annualized cost?

Because cost is only one side of the decision. A business might use fast funding to cover a short gap, secure inventory or take on work that would otherwise be lost, and the return on that use can outweigh the cost. The broker's job is not to make that decision for them, but to make sure they understand the cost in dollars and in time before they decide.
