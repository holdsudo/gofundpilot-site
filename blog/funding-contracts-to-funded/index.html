From Contract to Funded: Closing the Last Mile

An accepted offer is not a funded deal. The stretch between them is where files stall, merchants get cold feet and other brokers slip in. Run it like a checklist.

The merchant said yes at eleven. By three, the contract still has not been signed, the funder wants a voided check nobody asked for earlier, and the merchant has stopped answering because another broker called with a bigger number. The deal you spent a week building is now a race you might lose in its last hundred metres.

The final stretch from accepted offer to funded deal is short on paper and fragile in practice. It involves the merchant, the funder's closing team and your broker, all of whom need to do specific things in a specific order. Floors that close reliably treat this stage as its own process, with its own owner, steps and clock.

Pre-flight summary

  • Treat the close as its own pipeline with stages you can see and time.
  • Collect predictable closing stipulations when the offer is accepted.
  • Prepare merchants for verification calls, but never script their answers.
  • Anticipate recurring delays and give every accepted deal one accountable owner.
  • Confirm the net amount landed and check in after funding.

Leg 01Map the stages before you try to speed them up

Every funder's closing process differs a little, but most follow a similar shape. Knowing that shape lets you predict what comes next and prepare the merchant for it, instead of reacting to each request as a surprise.

Give each of these its own pipeline stage so you can see at a glance where every accepted deal sits and how long it has been there. A deal that has sat in contract out for two days is a different conversation from one that went out an hour ago.

  1. Offer accepted: the merchant has chosen an offer and understood its terms.
  2. Contract requested and sent: the funder issues the agreement for signature.
  3. Contract signed: the merchant signs and returns any requested items.
  4. Closing stipulations: final documents such as a voided check, identification or updated statements.
  5. Verification: the funder confirms details, often by phone, with the merchant.
  6. Funding call and approval: final checks before the funder releases the money.
  7. Funded: money sent, confirmation received and recorded on the deal.

Leg 02Collect the closing stips before the contract

The most common delay is a request the merchant could have satisfied days ago. A voided check, a copy of the owner's identification, proof of ownership, the latest few days of bank activity. If you know your funders ask for these at closing, ask the merchant for them when the offer is accepted, not when the funder emails.

A branded merchant portal makes this easier on both sides. In FundPilot, merchants upload documents through a portal carrying your shop's brand, and the files land on the deal, so nobody hunts through email for an attachment sent from a personal address.

  • Voided check or bank letter for the receiving account
  • Clear photo identification for each owner
  • Proof of business ownership if not already in the file
  • Most recent bank activity, as each funder requires
  • Landlord or other contact details if the funder verifies them

Leg 03Prepare the merchant for the verification call

Many funders call the merchant before funding to confirm identity, business details and that they understand the agreement. Merchants who are not expecting this call often miss it, or answer it cautiously because they are not sure who is calling. Either can delay funding by a day.

Tell the merchant the call is coming, roughly when, what it is for and what they will be asked. Encourage them to answer honestly and to ask questions on the call if any part of the agreement is unclear. Never coach a merchant to give a particular answer. The call exists to confirm the deal is accurate, and a broker who interferes with it puts the merchant and the deal at risk.

If the merchant raises a question you cannot answer, such as the meaning of a specific contract clause, point them to the funder or to their own adviser rather than guessing.

Tell the merchant the call is coming and why. Never tell them what to say on it.

Leg 04Common delays and what to do about each

Delays at this stage tend to repeat, which is good news: repeated problems can be anticipated. The trick is noticing them early rather than finding out at the end of the day.

A new position appears on the latest bank activity. A funder may re-price or decline. Tell the merchant directly, explain what it changes and do not try to work around it.

Signer mismatch. The person who signed is not the owner on file, or ownership percentages do not match. Resolve it before resending anything.

The merchant goes quiet. Often it means they are comparing another offer or have second thoughts about the payment. Ask plainly. If they have changed their mind, that is their right, and a respectful exit preserves the relationship for later.

The funder goes quiet. Escalate to your relationship contact at the funder rather than resubmitting or emailing three different people.

Leg 05Keep one person accountable for the close

Closing goes wrong when everyone assumes someone else is following up. Assign one owner per accepted deal, often the broker but sometimes a dedicated closer or processor on larger floors, and make the owner visible on the deal record.

That owner works from a clear checklist and a clock. A short morning review of every deal in a closing stage, sorted by time in stage, catches most stalls while they are still recoverable. FundPilot's AI assistant can answer questions like which accepted deals have been waiting on stips for more than a day from live CRM data, so the review does not depend on someone building a report first.

Leg 06After the money moves

Funded is a milestone, not an ending. Confirm the funds reached the merchant's account and that the amount matches the net figure they were told. Record the funded amount, payback, payment schedule and commission on the deal.

Then call the merchant. A short check-in a few days after funding, asking whether everything arrived as expected and whether they have questions about the first payments, is the beginning of the renewal relationship and costs almost nothing. It is also, if you will forgive the cockpit image, the moment you confirm the landing before taxiing to the gate.

Questions from the floor

What is a verification call in business funding?

A verification call is a call from the funder to the merchant, usually close to funding, to confirm identity, business details and that the merchant understands the agreement they signed. It protects both sides against errors and fraud. Brokers should tell the merchant to expect it and explain its purpose, but should never suggest what the merchant should say during the call.

Why do deals stall after the contract is signed?

The usual reasons are missing closing documents, a change on the merchant's latest bank activity, ownership or signer discrepancies, a missed verification call, or slow response from the funder. Most of these are predictable. Collecting likely documents early, warning the merchant about verification and reviewing closing-stage deals daily by time in stage prevents the majority of stalls.

What should a broker do if the merchant backs out at contract?

Ask what changed and listen. Sometimes it is a misunderstanding about the payment or net amount that can be clarified honestly. Sometimes the merchant simply decided they do not need the funding, or prefers another offer. Respect the decision, record the reason on the deal, and keep the door open. Pressuring a merchant at this stage damages trust and rarely produces a deal worth having.
