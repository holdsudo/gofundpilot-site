Handling the Five Objections Every Merchant Raises

Rate, daily payment, I'll think about it, my bank said no, and I already have a position. Five honest responses that respect the merchant and protect the deal.

By the FundPilot teamUpdated October 15, 2026

  7. Questions

You have heard all five of them this week. The merchant likes the idea of funding until they hear the cost. Or they nod along until the word daily comes up. Or they say they need to think about it in the tone that means they will not pick up next time. Or they tell you their bank already turned them down, as if that settles it. Or, halfway through qualification, they mention a position they took two months ago.

Objections are not obstacles to talk past. Each one is the merchant telling you what they are worried about, and most of them are reasonable worries. The brokers who handle them well are not the ones with the slickest comebacks. They are the ones who answer honestly, ask a good follow-up question, and are willing to say this might not be right for you when it is true. Here is how to approach each of the five.

Pre-flight summary

  • Acknowledge, ask, answer honestly, check, then agree on a next step.
  • Answer cost and payment objections with the merchant's own numbers.
  • Turn I'll think about it into a question and a scheduled follow-up.
  • A bank decline is information, not a promise of approval elsewhere.
  • Treat existing positions with care and never help hide one.

Leg 01A pattern that works for every objection

Before the specifics, one structure applies to all of them. It slows the conversation down at the moment a broker is most tempted to speed it up, and that pause is usually what makes the merchant trust the answer.

  1. Acknowledge the concern in plain words, without arguing
  2. Ask a question to understand what is behind it
  3. Answer honestly with specifics, including the downside
  4. Check whether the answer addressed the real worry
  5. Agree on a next step, even if that step is stepping back

Leg 02The rate is too high

This is often the right reaction. Short-term business funding can be expensive compared with a bank loan, and a merchant who notices that is paying attention. Do not argue the point. Help them see the full cost clearly and compare it with what the funding is for.

Explain the total amount they would repay, the remittance amount and frequency, and roughly how long it would take at their current deposit levels. Then ask what the funds will do. Say a contractor needs capital to take on a job worth considerably more than the cost of the funding; the math may work. Say a merchant wants to cover payroll in a slow month with no plan for the next one; the cost may make things worse, and saying so is part of your job.

Never call an advance a loan to make the cost sound familiar, and never compare it to a rate it is not. If disclosure requirements apply to your offers in the merchant's state, make sure what you say lines up with the disclosure they receive.

Leg 03The daily payment scares me

This objection is really a cash-flow question: will money leave my account on days when not enough comes in? Answer it with their own numbers. Look at their average daily deposits and their lowest balances, and compare them with the proposed remittance.

If the payment would sit comfortably within a normal day's deposits, show them that. If it would push their balance close to zero on slow days, take that seriously. Ask whether weekly remittance is available from the lenders who would consider their file, and explain how reconciliation or adjustment provisions generally work if their contract includes them, without promising outcomes that depend on the funder.

Answer the daily payment objection with the merchant's own deposits, not with reassurance.

Leg 04I need to think about it

Sometimes this means exactly what it says, and pushing is both unfair and counterproductive. Sometimes it means there is an unspoken objection the merchant does not want to raise. The useful response is a question, not pressure.

Try: that makes sense, it is a real decision. Is there something specific you want to think through, like the payment or the total cost, or someone you need to talk it over with? That often surfaces the real concern. If it does not, agree on a specific follow-up time, send a short written summary of what you discussed, and call when you said you would.

FundPilot's AI assistant can draft that summary email from the deal record for the broker to review, edit and send; nothing goes out without a person confirming it. The merchant gets a clear recap and the broker does not lose twenty minutes writing it.

Leg 05My bank already said no

Treat this as information, not a dead end. Ask what the bank cited, if the merchant knows: time in business, credit, collateral, documentation. Banks and alternative funders weigh different factors, and a business that does not fit a bank's criteria may still be a reasonable fit for funding based on its receivables and deposit history.

Be careful not to oversell. A bank decline is not a reason someone else will approve, and the same issues may come up again. Explain what alternative funders typically look at, ask for statements so you can give an honest view, and set the expectation that an offer depends on underwriting.

Leg 06I already have a position

This one deserves the most care. Thank the merchant for telling you; it will show up on their statements anyway, and hearing it early lets you give honest advice. Ask how much is outstanding, the remittance amount, who the funder is, and why they want more.

Then think about whether additional funding would genuinely help. Some funders consider second positions in specific circumstances, and some merchants have legitimate needs alongside an existing advance. But a merchant already struggling with one remittance can be hurt badly by another. Never encourage a merchant to hide an existing position, and do not submit a file you would not be comfortable defending. Sometimes the best advice is to wait until the current advance is further paid down.

  • Confirm balance, remittance and funder for every existing position
  • Calculate combined remittance against average daily deposits
  • Check each lender's guidelines on additional positions before submitting
  • Record the conversation and your reasoning on the deal

Questions from the floor

Is it ever right to stop pursuing a merchant after an objection?

Yes. If the honest answer to the objection is that this funding would strain the business or does not fit what they need, say so and step back. Note the reason on the deal and, with their agreement, check in later when circumstances may have changed. Merchants remember brokers who were straight with them, and that reputation is worth more than one forced deal.

How do I explain a factor rate without calling it a loan?

Describe it as the total amount purchased relative to the amount funded. For example, explain that for a hypothetical advance, the merchant receives a set amount now and the funder collects an agreed larger amount from future receivables. Then translate that into the remittance amount and expected duration. Avoid loan vocabulary like interest or principal, which misdescribes how an advance is generally structured.

Should objection handling be scripted word for word?

No. Give brokers the structure and example phrasings, then let them answer in their own words. Merchants spot memorised rebuttals and push back harder. The only parts that should be fixed text are any required disclosures, which should be approved by qualified counsel. Review recordings to see which honest phrasings land well and share them with the floor.

How can managers see how objections are being handled?

Listen to recordings, ideally with searchable transcripts such as the ones FundPilot builds from every recorded call, so you can find the moments that matter. Searching a week of calls for phrases like think about it or already have shows how each broker responds and where coaching would help. Pair that with outcome data, such as how often those conversations still led to an application, rather than judging by tone alone.
