Restricted and Prohibited Industries: Why Lenders Decline Them

Some declines have nothing to do with the merchant's numbers. Knowing why certain industries are restricted saves time and spares merchants false hope.

The file is beautiful. Steady deposits, healthy balances, no existing positions, years in business. Then three funders decline within the hour, each with the same brief reason: industry. The merchant is confused and a little offended. You are confused too, because nobody told you this kind of business was a problem.

Industry restrictions are one of the least discussed parts of business funding, and one of the most predictable. Funders keep lists of industries they will not fund, or will only fund under tighter terms. The lists differ from funder to funder and change over time, but the reasoning behind them is fairly consistent. Understand the reasoning and you can predict most industry declines before you ever submit.

Pre-flight summary

  • Restricted industries get extra scrutiny; prohibited industries are not funded at all.
  • Funders restrict industries for legal, verification, dispute and reputational reasons.
  • Lists vary by funder and change over time, so keep them current.
  • Check industry at the first call, before collecting a full file.
  • Never misclassify a business to get around a restriction.

Leg 01Restricted is not the same as prohibited

Most funders work with roughly two tiers. Prohibited industries are ones the funder will not fund at all, regardless of the numbers. Restricted industries are ones the funder will consider, but with extra scrutiny, smaller amounts, shorter terms or additional documents.

The same industry can be prohibited at one funder, restricted at another and perfectly acceptable at a third. That is why a single decline tells you little, and why keeping each lender's current list on record matters so much.

Leg 02Why funders draw these lines

The reasons tend to fall into a handful of themes. Understanding them helps you explain a decline to a merchant without it sounding arbitrary or personal.

  • Legal or regulatory complexity, where the business operates in a space with unsettled or varying rules
  • Revenue that is hard to predict or verify, which makes future receivables hard to assess
  • High rates of chargebacks, disputes or refunds that can erode deposits quickly
  • Businesses whose revenue can stop abruptly, such as those relying on a single contract
  • Reputational concerns or restrictions imposed by the funder's own banking or investor partners
  • Industries where a funder has simply had poor experience in the past

Leg 03Categories that commonly appear on lists

Every funder's list is different, so treat this as a general picture rather than a rulebook. Industries that frequently show up as restricted or prohibited include businesses in legally sensitive areas, such as firearms, adult entertainment, gambling and cannabis-related operations; financial businesses, such as other lenders, check cashers and collections agencies; businesses with long or uncertain revenue cycles, such as some real estate investors and early-stage startups; and some industries with a history of high disputes, such as certain travel, telemarketing or subscription businesses.

Other industries can be restricted at particular funders for reasons specific to them: trucking, construction, auto sales, nonprofits and certain professional services all appear on some lists and not others. The only reliable answer for any given funder is its current guidelines.

An industry decline is not a judgment of the merchant. It is a statement about the funder's appetite.

Leg 04Check industry before anything else

The most practical change you can make is to check industry first, before you collect a full file and before you set any expectations. Ask what the business does early in the first call, and ask follow-up questions when the answer is vague. "Retail" could mean a clothing boutique or a vape shop. "Consulting" could mean anything.

Then check the merchant's industry against your panel's current guidelines. In FundPilot, lender matching scores each file against the guidelines you have recorded for each lender, including industry exclusions, so restricted-industry files surface the funders who will actually look at them and quietly skip the ones that will not.

  1. Ask what the business sells, to whom and how it is paid.
  2. Clarify vague answers with specific follow-up questions.
  3. Record the industry on the deal accurately.
  4. Check it against each lender's current exclusions.
  5. Set expectations with the merchant before collecting the full file.

Leg 05Setting honest expectations with the merchant

If a merchant's industry is widely restricted, tell them early and plainly. Something like: "The type of business you run is one that many of our funders don't work with, and the ones who do may offer smaller amounts or ask for more paperwork. I'll check who fits, but I want you to know where we stand." Merchants handle honest news much better than a string of unexplained declines.

Never describe the business as something it is not to get past a filter. Classifying a dispensary-adjacent business as general retail, or a collections agency as consulting, misrepresents the file. Funders will often discover the truth from the statements or a website, and the consequences can be serious for the merchant and for you.

Leg 06Keeping your panel's lists current

Industry lists change as funders adjust appetite, take on new capital partners or respond to their own results. A list you saved last year may be out of date. Ask lender reps periodically whether their exclusions have changed, and update your records the same day.

When a rep tells you an industry is now off their list, or newly back on it, write down the date and who told you. A guideline with a date attached is one the whole floor can trust, and an undated one is a guess.

Keep those notes where the whole floor can see them. FundPilot stores lender guidelines centrally and threads lender replies onto each deal, so when a funder declines for industry, that reply is visible and can prompt an update to the guidelines for everyone, rather than staying in one broker's inbox.

Questions from the floor

Why would a profitable business be declined because of its industry?

Because industry restrictions reflect the funder's risk appetite, legal concerns or partner requirements, not the individual merchant's performance. A funder that has decided not to work with a category will usually decline every business in it, however strong. That is frustrating for good merchants, but it is predictable, which is why checking industry early saves everyone time and disappointment.

Are industry rules the same at every funder?

No. Lists vary considerably and change over time. One funder may exclude an industry entirely while another considers it with tighter terms and a third has no restriction at all. Always confirm against each lender's current guidelines rather than relying on general lists or past deals. Where an industry also involves legal questions, the merchant should confirm their own position with qualified counsel.

Can a business with mixed activities be classified by its main line?

Often a business does several things, and describing it by its primary revenue source is reasonable when that is accurate. Disclose the other activities too, especially if any fall into a restricted category. Let the funder decide how to classify it. Presenting a partial picture to avoid a restriction is misrepresentation, even if every individual statement you make is technically true.
