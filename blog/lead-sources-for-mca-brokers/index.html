Evaluating Lead Sources for MCA and Business-Lending Brokers

Every vendor's leads look great on the sales call. The only scorecard that counts is the one you build from your own dials, submissions and funded files.

Most shops pick lead sources the way people pick restaurants on a trip: a recommendation from someone down the hall, a vendor with a confident pitch, a deal on volume that seemed too good to pass up. Three months later the floor is grumbling that the leads are junk, the vendor says your brokers are not working them, and nobody has the data to settle it.

The fix is not finding the perfect vendor. It is tagging every lead with where it came from, then following each source all the way to funded and paid. Once you can see cost and outcome side by side, the arguments get shorter.

Below is a plain look at the common source types, what each tends to be good and bad at, and a scorecard you can keep yourself.

Pre-flight summary

  • Tag every lead with its source at creation and lock the field.
  • Judge sources on cost per funded deal, not cost per lead.
  • Get consent language reviewed by counsel before calling or texting any purchased data.
  • Split new sources across several brokers so you can tell data problems from coaching problems.
  • Set your stop rule before the test starts, and scale winning sources gradually.

Leg 01The five source types on most floors

Each type has a different shape. None is universally good or bad; they suit different brokers and different stages of a shop's growth.

  • Inbound web inquiries: merchants who found you and asked. Usually the highest intent and the most time-sensitive, because they are often shopping several shops at once.
  • Aged data: older inquiries resold after the original buyer worked them. Cheaper per record, much lower contact rates, and consent and freshness need checking before any outreach.
  • Live transfers: a vendor qualifies the merchant on the phone and hands them to your broker. Expensive per call; quality depends heavily on how the vendor screens.
  • Referrals: accountants, equipment sellers, other brokers and past merchants. Slow to build, often the best conversion, and the relationship needs maintaining.
  • UCC-based lists: businesses with public filings suggesting existing financing. Useful for targeting merchants with experience of funding, but they invite stacking conversations that need careful, responsible handling.

Leg 02Ask vendors the questions they would rather skip

Before you buy, find out how the data was collected and what the merchant actually agreed to. Ask what the opt-in form said, whether it named your company or a category of companies, and how many other buyers receive the same record. Ask for a sample of the consent capture, not a description of it.

Consent rules for calls and texts are a legal question, and requirements change. Have qualified counsel review what a source's consent language does and does not cover before you dial or text anyone from it. A cheap list that creates exposure is not cheap.

Also ask what happens with bad records. Disconnected numbers, duplicates and merchants who never asked for funding should be creditable under a clear return policy, with a deadline you can meet.

Leg 03Build a scorecard from your own numbers

Industry averages tell you little about your floor. What you want is each source measured through the same funnel, over the same period, worked by the same people. Tag the source on every record at the moment it is created and never let a broker overwrite it.

Then track these stages for each source, monthly. In FundPilot, custom pipeline stages and call logging on the deal make this a report rather than a spreadsheet exercise, but the logic works anywhere.

  1. Records received and cost paid.
  2. Contact rate: records where a real conversation happened.
  3. Application rate: conversations that produced a signed application and statements.
  4. Submission rate: files actually sent to funders.
  5. Funded rate and total commission earned.
  6. Cost per funded deal: total spend divided by funded files from that source.

Cost per lead flatters cheap sources. Cost per funded deal is the number that pays the rent.

Leg 04A worked comparison

Say you spend the same amount in a month on two sources. Source A delivers 400 aged records; Source B delivers 40 live transfers. On cost per lead, A looks ten times better. Now follow them through. Your brokers reach 48 merchants from A and get 6 applications, of which 2 fund. From B they speak to 36, get 11 applications, and 4 fund. B costs half as much per funded deal, and it ate far fewer broker hours.

These figures are invented to show the arithmetic, not to predict anything. Your own numbers could come out the other way, especially if you have a strong reactivation process for aged data. The point is that you only know once both sources are measured to the end.

Leg 05Separate source quality from broker performance

A source can look weak because it went to your newest broker, or strong because your best closer worked it. When you test a new source, split it across several brokers and compare each person's results on that source with their results on your baseline source. If everyone does worse, it is probably the source. If one person does worse, it is probably coaching.

Listening to a sample of calls helps here. FundPilot turns call recordings into searchable transcripts with summaries, so a manager can search a new source's calls for phrases like never applied or wrong number and see quickly whether the problem is the data or the pitch.

Leg 06When to cut, keep or scale a source

Give a new source enough volume and time to read honestly; a single week rarely tells you much, because funding cycles take longer than that. Decide in advance what result would make you stop, so the decision is not driven by whichever broker complained loudest.

Keep a referral program even if its volume is small. Those relationships tend to compound, and a referral partner who sends one good file a month is worth looking after. Scale a paid source slowly, because quality often changes when a vendor has to fill a bigger order.

Questions from the floor

What is the best lead source for a new MCA broker?

There is no single answer. Many brokers start with a mix: a modest inbound or live-transfer budget for steady conversations, plus deliberate work on referral relationships that pay off later. The right mix depends on your capacity to work leads quickly and your budget. Measure each source to funded deals before committing more money to it.

Are aged leads worth buying?

They can be, if your process suits them: low cost per record, patient multi-touch outreach, and careful checks on consent and freshness. Expect far lower contact rates than fresh inquiries. Before any outreach, have counsel review whether the original consent covers your calls and texts. Measure aged data on cost per funded deal, not cost per record.

How do I check whether a lead vendor's consent is valid?

Ask for an actual example of the opt-in page and the record of each consumer's or merchant's agreement, including the date, the wording shown and which companies were named. Then have qualified counsel review it against current requirements. This article is general information, not legal advice, and consent rules for calls and texts change over time.

How long should I test a new lead source?

Long enough to see files through to funding, which usually means more than a few days. Agree a fixed budget and period in advance, split the leads across several brokers, and decide what outcome would make you stop or continue. Writing that down beforehand keeps the decision based on results rather than impressions.
