Keeping Lenders Happy: Relationship Habits That Get Files Approved

Underwriters remember which brokers send clean, honest files. That memory decides who gets a call back on a borderline deal, and who waits until Monday.

By the FundPilot teamUpdated October 15, 2026

Route

  1. Send the file you would want to receive
  2. Disclose the hard parts up front
  3. Communicate like a colleague, not a chaser
  4. Respect their decisions, including the declines
  5. Make it a floor standard, not a personal style
  6. Close the loop after funding
  7. Questions

Ask any underwriter about the brokers they work with and you will hear two lists, even if they are too polite to name names. One list is the brokers whose files they open first, because the statements are complete, the application matches the bank activity and nothing surprising turns up later. The other is everyone else.

Being on the first list does not come from lunches or holiday gifts. It comes from dozens of small, repeated habits that make an underwriter's job easier and their decisions safer. None of them are complicated. What makes them rare is consistency, across every broker on your floor and every file they send.

Pre-flight summary

  • Clean, complete, consistent submissions are the foundation of every funder relationship.
  • Disclose difficult details up front with context; never let an underwriter discover them.
  • Communicate briefly and specifically, and keep replies on the deal.
  • Treat declines as information and revisit only when something material changes.
  • Make relationship habits a floor-wide standard with a named owner per funder.

Leg 01Send the file you would want to receive

Think about what happens on the other side when your submission arrives. An underwriter opens it, checks the application against the statements, looks for positions, checks the industry, and starts forming a view. Every missing page or inconsistency stops that process and generates a question back to you.

A clean submission is complete, legible and internally consistent. The application's revenue matches what the statements show, the owner information matches the identification, and any existing positions are disclosed rather than discovered. FundPilot's statement underwriting links each extracted figure, deposits, balances, NSFs and positions, to the page it came from, which makes it straightforward to send a summary the underwriter can verify in seconds.

  • Complete statements for the requested period, every page
  • Application fields filled and matching the documents
  • All known positions listed, with balances where available
  • A short cover note explaining anything unusual
  • Files named so an underwriter can tell what each one is

Leg 02Disclose the hard parts up front

Every file has something an underwriter will not like. A recent run of negative days, a position the merchant only mentioned on the second call, a dip in revenue during a slow season. The temptation is to say nothing and hope it is overlooked. It will not be, and when it is found the question changes from whether the file works to whether you can be trusted.

Put the issue in the cover note with the context you have. Say a restaurant showed three NSFs in March because a large catering client paid late, and deposits recovered in April. Saying that plainly gives the underwriter a reason to look past it. Leaving it for them to find gives them a reason to doubt everything else in the file.

This is not just tactics. Concealing positions or misstating revenue is misrepresentation that puts the merchant at risk of a deal they cannot sustain, and it can expose your shop to real consequences.

An issue you disclose is a question about the file. An issue they discover is a question about you.

Leg 03Communicate like a colleague, not a chaser

Underwriters handle many files from many brokers. Messages that are short, specific and sent to the right person get answered faster than repeated requests for status. Ask a clear question, give the deal identifier and say what you need and by when.

Answer their questions the same way. When an underwriter asks for something, respond promptly, even if only to say the merchant is gathering it and when you expect it. Silence on your side looks like a file that is falling apart.

Keep replies on the deal. FundPilot threads lender replies onto the deal record, so anyone on your team picking up the file can see what was asked and answered without forwarding emails around.

Leg 04Respect their decisions, including the declines

A decline is information. Ask for the reason if it was not given, record it on the lender profile and move on. Arguing a decline, resubmitting the same file unchanged or sending it through a colleague to try again damages the relationship more than the lost deal is worth.

There is a respectful way to revisit. If something material changes, such as a position is paid off or new months of statements show recovery, it is reasonable to ask whether the funder would take another look. Lead with what changed, not with why they were wrong.

  1. Thank the underwriter and ask for the main reason if none was given.
  2. Record the reason on the lender record and the deal.
  3. Check whether the reason should have been caught at matching.
  4. Revisit only if something material changes, and say what changed.
  5. Tell the merchant honestly and move to the next appropriate funder.

Leg 05Make it a floor standard, not a personal style

A funder experiences your shop as a whole. One broker who sends careless files or argues every decline affects how every other broker's files are received. Relationship quality has to be a shared standard, taught during onboarding and reviewed regularly.

Practical ways to do that include a submission checklist every broker uses, a relationship owner for each funder who hears feedback first, and a regular look at outcomes by broker and by funder. Where a funder raises a concern about a particular broker, take it seriously and address it directly.

Leg 06Close the loop after funding

Relationships are mostly built during the deal, but they are kept between deals. Tell a funder when one of their merchants has a question you helped resolve, or when a merchant comes back for a renewal because the first deal went well. Pass along changes you notice that affect their book, handled appropriately and with the merchant's privacy respected.

And when something goes wrong on a deal you placed, such as a merchant struggling with payments, do not disappear. Help where you appropriately can. Funders notice which brokers stay involved when deals get difficult, and those are the brokers whose borderline files get a second look.

Questions from the floor

What makes a submission clean in the eyes of an underwriter?

A clean submission is complete, legible and consistent. It includes every requested statement page, an application whose figures match the bank activity, all known positions disclosed, correct owner details and a short note explaining anything unusual. The underwriter should be able to start their review without asking for anything, and nothing they find later should contradict what you told them.

Should a broker push back on a decline?

Arguing a decline usually hurts the relationship. It is reasonable to ask for the reason and to record it. If something material later changes, such as a paid-off position or several stronger months of deposits, you can ask whether the funder would reconsider, leading with what is new. Resubmitting an unchanged file or routing it through a colleague is not a good practice.

Who on a funding floor should own lender relationships?

Many shops assign each funder to a senior broker or manager who becomes the main contact for that relationship. That person hears feedback, tracks guideline changes and handles escalations, while every broker still follows the same submission standards. Recording the owner and their notes on the lender record keeps the relationship intact if people change roles or leave the shop.
