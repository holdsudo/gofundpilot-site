What a Funding Manager Should See Every Morning

A morning dashboard should answer one question in under five minutes: where do I need to step in today? Here is how to build one around exceptions.

By the FundPilot teamUpdated October 15, 2026

The typical sales dashboard was designed for a board meeting. Big tiles with month-to-date funded volume, a bar chart of submissions by broker, a pipeline value number that grows impressively because nobody closes dead deals. It looks great on a wall-mounted screen. At 8:45 in the morning, with the floor about to start dialing, it tells a funding manager almost nothing about what to do.

A manager's morning view has a different job. It should point at the specific deals, leads and people that need attention today, and it should be quiet about everything that is fine. This guide covers how to design that view: what to show, what to leave off, how to spot coaching needs before they become performance problems, and how to keep the dashboard trustworthy.

Pre-flight summary

  • Build the morning view as a sorted list of exceptions, not totals.
  • Keep only panels that change what the manager does today.
  • Watch leading indicators against each broker's own baseline.
  • Move monthly and owner-level numbers to a separate report.
  • Clean data upstream or the dashboard turns into wallpaper.

Leg 01Exceptions over totals

A total tells you how things are going in aggregate. An exception tells you what is going wrong and where. Managers act on exceptions. Nobody can do anything useful with the fact that the floor has ninety deals in Submitted, but everybody can act on the fact that eleven of them have had no lender reply in three business days.

Design the morning dashboard as a list of problems, sorted by urgency, with each item linked to the deal or person concerned. When everything is fine, the dashboard should look nearly empty. That emptiness is the most reassuring thing a manager can see.

Leg 02The five panels that earn their place

Every panel on the morning view should pass a simple test: if this panel shows something, does the manager do something different today? These five usually pass.

  • Untouched leads: new leads with no contact attempt, oldest first, with the assigned broker
  • Stuck deals: deals past their stage time limit, grouped by stage and owner
  • Lender silence: submissions with no response past your agreed window
  • Closing today: contracts out and stipulations due, with the next action and its owner
  • Broker anomalies: anyone whose activity or conversion this week is far from their own normal

Leg 03Reading coaching signals

The fifth panel is the one most dashboards miss. Performance problems almost always show up in leading indicators before they show up in fundings. A broker whose contact rate has slipped, whose applications are piling up without documents, or whose approved deals keep failing to close is telling you something specific.

Say one broker's approval rate is healthy but their approval-to-fund rate has dropped over a couple of weeks. Their files are good; something is going wrong at the offer presentation. That is a precise coaching conversation: listen to their last few offer calls together. Compare that with a broker whose contact rate fell sharply. That might be a dialing-time problem, a caller ID issue, or a personal matter worth a quiet check-in.

Compare each broker with their own baseline, not with the floor's top performer. Someone who is consistently average and suddenly drops matters more than someone who is consistently average.

Leg 04What to leave off the morning view

Some useful numbers belong somewhere else. Month-to-date funded volume, lender mix, commission totals and lead-source return all matter, but they belong on a weekly or monthly owner's report. Putting them on the morning view dilutes the exceptions and invites managers to spend the first hour admiring or worrying about numbers they cannot change before lunch.

Leave off any metric that is easy to game when it is watched daily, such as raw dial counts. Watching them every morning nudges brokers toward empty activity.

Leg 05Keeping the dashboard honest

A dashboard is only as reliable as the data behind it. If stages are used inconsistently, if calls are not logged, or if lost deals are never closed, the exception lists fill with noise and managers stop trusting them. Within weeks, the dashboard becomes wallpaper.

The fix is mostly upstream. Event-based stages with clear exit criteria, automatic logging of calls and texts, and a rule that stale deals are closed with a reason all keep the data clean. In FundPilot, every call from the built-in dialer is logged to the deal automatically and lender replies thread onto the deal, so the lender-silence and stuck-deal panels reflect what actually happened rather than what someone remembered to type.

  1. Define each stage with an exit criterion and a time limit
  2. Make call, text and email logging automatic rather than manual
  3. Close deals past a maximum age with a loss reason
  4. Spot-check five random deals a week against the dashboard
  5. Remove any panel nobody acted on in the last month

Leg 06From dashboard to action in five minutes

The morning routine should be short. Open the view, scan each panel top to bottom, click into anything that needs a decision, and write the two or three items for the daily huddle. If it takes longer than five or ten minutes, the dashboard is showing too much.

Questions that do not fit a panel can be asked directly. FundPilot's AI assistant answers from live CRM data, so a manager can ask which submissions from last week still have no reply from any lender and get a list without building a new report. Questions from the floor

Should brokers see the same dashboard as managers?

Brokers benefit from their own version: their untouched leads, their stuck deals and their closing list, without the team-wide anomaly panel. Role-based access lets you show each person what is relevant to them. A broker who starts the day with a clear personal exception list often fixes problems before the manager ever needs to raise them.

How often should dashboard panels change?

Review the panels monthly. If a panel has been empty for weeks, the problem it tracked may be solved, and the space could serve something else. If a panel is always full and nobody acts on it, either the threshold is wrong or the issue needs a structural fix rather than daily attention. Small, deliberate changes keep the view useful without confusing the team.

What is a good time limit for each stage?

It depends on your floor, lead mix and lenders, so set limits from your own history rather than borrowing someone else's. Look at how long deals that eventually funded spent in each stage, and set the limit a little beyond the typical time. Deals past that point are worth a look. Revisit the limits once you have a few months of cleaner data.

Do I need a separate business-intelligence tool?

Not for the morning view. If your CRM records stages, timestamps, calls and lender responses consistently, exception lists can be built from saved views and filters. Owners who want deeper analysis across months may want more, but most of what a floor manager needs every morning comes directly from clean CRM data.
