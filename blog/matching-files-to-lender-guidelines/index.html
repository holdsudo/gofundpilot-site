Matching Files to Lender Guidelines Before You Submit

Most declines are predictable. Checking a file against each funder's knockouts and preferences first saves everyone a round trip and keeps your submissions credible.

A decline that arrives three hours after submission, citing a restricted industry that was printed on the funder's guideline sheet, is a decline you sent yourself. The merchant waited, the underwriter spent time on a file they were never going to fund, and your shop's name went on a pile of submissions that did not read the rules.

Matching before submitting is the unglamorous habit that fixes this. It is part checklist, part judgment: knock out the funders who cannot take the file, then rank the rest by how well the file fits what they actually want. Done consistently, it shortens time to offer and quietly raises how seriously funders take your submissions.

Pre-flight summary

  • Treat guideline sheets as two lists: hard knockouts and softer preferences.
  • Run knockouts before anything else so no file goes where it cannot be funded.
  • Score surviving funders consistently so every broker reaches the same shortlist.
  • Match on true revenue and an accurate position count, never on rounded or adjusted figures.
  • Use declines and approvals to keep each funder's guideline record honest.

Leg 01What a guideline sheet really tells you

A funder's guideline sheet is a summary of the files they are built to approve. The common fields are minimum time in business, minimum monthly revenue or deposits, maximum number of open positions, minimum credit score if they pull it, restricted industries, restricted states, minimum and maximum funding amounts, and required documents.

Read it as two lists. Some items are hard lines the funder will not cross, such as an industry they are not permitted to fund or a state they do not operate in. Others are preferences that can bend when the rest of the file is strong. Knowing which is which comes from the sheet itself, from the funder's rep, and from your own history of what they have approved.

Write down the source and date of every guideline you rely on. A rule a rep mentioned on a call last year is weaker evidence than a sheet they sent this month.

Leg 02Run knockouts first

Knockout criteria are the fastest filter you have. If a file fails even one, that funder is off the list for this deal, regardless of how strong everything else looks. Apply them before anyone thinks about pricing or preference.

Say a merchant is a used-car dealership in business for fourteen months, showing roughly $48,000 a month in deposits, with two open advances. Any funder that restricts auto sales, requires two years in business, or caps positions at one is out. What remains is your real list, and it may be short. That is useful information on its own: a short list tells you to set expectations with the merchant early.

  • Industry restrictions and prohibited business types
  • States or regions the funder does not serve
  • Time in business below the stated minimum
  • Deposits or revenue below the stated floor
  • More open positions than the funder accepts
  • Recent defaults or other disqualifiers the funder lists

Leg 03Score the funders that survive

After knockouts, rank the remaining funders by fit. A simple scoring approach gives points for each guideline the file clears comfortably, fewer points where it only just clears, and notes the soft concerns, such as a high count of negative days that a particular funder is known to dislike.

The numbers matter less than the consistency. Two brokers looking at the same file should arrive at roughly the same shortlist. FundPilot's lender matching does this scoring against the guidelines stored on each lender record, so every broker starts from the same ranked list and can see why a funder scored the way it did before choosing where to send the file.

Scoring also shows you which tier the file belongs in. If it clears your first-tier funders comfortably, there is rarely a good reason for it to go anywhere else first.

Leg 04Get the inputs right before you match

Matching is only as good as the numbers you feed it. If monthly revenue is taken straight from total deposits, it may include transfers between the merchant's own accounts, loan proceeds or refunds, all of which inflate the figure. A file that looks like it clears a $40,000 minimum may really be closer to $31,000 once those are backed out.

The same goes for positions. Count every active advance you can see on the statements, including ones the merchant forgot to mention. FundPilot's statement underwriting pulls deposits, balances, NSFs and existing positions from the uploaded statements and links each figure to the page it came from, which makes it easier to match on true numbers and to answer an underwriter's questions later.

  1. Confirm the business type and state against restricted lists.
  2. Calculate true monthly revenue, excluding transfers and non-sales deposits.
  3. Count open positions from the statements, not only the application.
  4. Note average daily balance, negative days and NSFs for the period.
  5. Check that required documents are in the file and readable.
  6. Only then run knockouts and scoring.

Leg 05When the file sits on the edge

Some files miss a guideline by a little. Ten months in business against a twelve-month minimum, with otherwise strong deposits. In those cases, do not submit and hope. Call or message the funder's rep, describe the file honestly, and ask whether it is worth sending. A short conversation respects their time and often gets a clearer answer than a formal submission would.

Never adjust a file to fit a guideline. Rounding time in business up, leaving an existing position off the application or presenting a transfer as revenue is misrepresentation, it puts the merchant at risk, and it is the fastest way to lose a funder's trust permanently.

Leg 06Feed the results back into your guidelines

Every submission produces information. When a funder declines a file that matched their sheet, note the reason. When one approves something just outside their stated lines, note that too. Over a few months those notes turn a static guideline sheet into an accurate picture of how each funder actually behaves.

Make that review part of the routine rather than something done when a broker complains. A monthly look at declines by funder, alongside their stated guidelines, usually surfaces one or two records that need updating.

Questions from the floor

What is a knockout criterion in business funding?

A knockout criterion is a guideline a funder will not bend, such as a restricted industry, an unsupported state or a firm minimum time in business. If a file fails one, that funder will decline it regardless of the rest of the file. Checking knockouts first removes funders who cannot take the deal, so the merchant is not left waiting on submissions that were never going to produce an offer.

Is it ever acceptable to submit a file that misses a guideline?

Sometimes, if the funder agrees in advance. When a file narrowly misses a soft guideline and is strong elsewhere, contact the funder's rep, describe the file accurately and ask whether they want to see it. If they say yes, submit with a note explaining the exception. What is never acceptable is changing information on the file so it appears to meet the guideline.

How does guideline scoring differ from just picking favorite funders?

Picking favorites relies on habit and memory, so different brokers send similar files to different places and some good fits are never considered. Scoring checks the file against every funder's recorded guidelines the same way each time, then ranks them. It makes the shortlist explainable to a manager or a merchant, and it surfaces funders a broker might not have thought of.
