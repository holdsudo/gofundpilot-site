Power Dialer vs. Click-to-Call: Choosing for Your Floor

One mode gets more people on the phone per hour. The other gives your broker a breath to prepare. Most floors need both, used for different jobs.

Walk a funding floor at ten in the morning and you can usually tell which dialing mode each broker is using without looking at a screen. The broker on a power dialer has a rhythm: hang up, a few seconds of notes, next ring. The broker on click-to-call is reading a deal record, checking statements, then picking up the phone with a specific question ready.

Neither is wrong. Arguments about which is better usually come from people who have only seen one used badly. The real question is which mode fits which kind of call, and what guardrails each needs.

Here is how to think about it in practical terms, including the compliance questions that come with any automated dialing.

Pre-flight summary

  • Power dialing removes dead time between calls; click-to-call gives room to prepare.
  • Choose the mode by list type, not by which broker prefers which.
  • Dialing more lines than available brokers creates silent answers and potential compliance questions.
  • Have counsel review your dialing setup and consent records before any automated dialing.
  • Measure conversations and next steps, not raw dial counts.

Leg 01What each mode actually does

Click-to-call is the simplest: the broker opens a record and clicks the number, and the call is placed from the browser or app and logged to the deal. Nothing happens until a person decides to dial that specific merchant.

A power dialer works through a list for the broker. When one call ends, it moves to the next record and dials, typically one line at a time per broker, so a person is ready when the merchant answers. The broker's job shifts from finding the next number to handling conversations and writing quick notes.

Some systems go further and dial several lines per broker at once, predicting who will answer. That raises the chance a merchant picks up and nobody is there, which is the abandoned-call problem covered below. FundPilot's dialer offers both click-to-call and a power dialer with auto-dial, with every call logged to the deal either way.

Leg 02Throughput versus quality, honestly

Say a broker on click-to-call makes forty dials in a morning and has nine conversations. On a power dialer the same broker might make well over a hundred dials and have twenty-odd conversations. Those numbers are illustrative, but the shape is common: the dialer removes dead time between calls.

What it does not do is make each conversation better. If the list is a mix of first-time inquiries and merchants halfway through underwriting, a broker blasting through it will arrive at sensitive calls unprepared. More conversations at lower quality can mean fewer files funded, not more.

A dialer speeds up the gaps between calls. It does nothing for the call itself.

Leg 03Abandoned calls and the autodialer question

When a system dials more lines than there are free brokers, some merchants answer to silence or a delay. That is a poor experience at best, and depending on how your calls are placed and to whom, it may raise regulatory questions about abandoned calls and about whether your equipment counts as an automatic telephone dialing system for consent purposes.

These are legal questions with technical answers that turn on how your system works and current interpretations, which have shifted over time. This article does not settle them. Have qualified counsel review your dialing setup, your consent records and your lists before running any automated dialing, and confirm current requirements rather than relying on what another shop told you. A one-to-one power dialing setup, where a broker is always ready for each call placed, avoids the silent-answer problem by design, but it does not remove the need for consent and Do Not Call checks.

Leg 04Matching the mode to the call

The easiest way to use both modes well is to decide by list, not by broker preference. Some call types benefit from momentum; others need preparation.

  • Power dialer: fresh inbound inquiries with documented consent, worked quickly in a single session.
  • Power dialer: callback lists where merchants asked to be called and the purpose of each call is the same.
  • Click-to-call: merchants in underwriting, where you need statements and notes open before you speak.
  • Click-to-call: offer presentations and renewal conversations, which deserve full attention.
  • Click-to-call: any record where consent is unclear, until someone has checked it.

Leg 05Setting up a power dialing session properly

A good session starts before anyone dials. The list should be clean, scoped and checked, and brokers should know what the call is for.

  1. Build the list from a saved filter, such as new inquiries from the last 24 hours with consent recorded.
  2. Remove records on your internal Do Not Call list and any that require a registry check under your process.
  3. Confirm the session respects the merchants' local calling hours.
  4. Give brokers a one-line purpose for the session and a short opener.
  5. Allow a few seconds of wrap-up after each call for notes and a next step.
  6. Review connect rates and outcomes at the end, not just dial counts.

Leg 06How managers should read the numbers

Dial counts are the easiest number to inflate and the least useful on their own. Look at conversations per hour, applications started, and how many of those conversations produced a clear next step. Compare brokers within the same mode, because comparing a power dialing session with a morning of underwriting calls tells you nothing.

Listening matters as much as counting. FundPilot turns call recordings into searchable transcripts with summaries, so a manager can review a handful of power-dialed calls from each broker and check that speed has not crowded out listening. If the summaries keep showing merchants asking the same question the broker did not answer, that is a coaching point, not a dialer setting.

Questions from the floor

Is a power dialer the same as a predictive dialer?

Not quite. A power dialer usually places one call at a time per broker and moves to the next record when a call ends. A predictive dialer places several calls at once and connects whoever answers to an available broker, which can leave some people answering to silence. How either is treated for consent purposes is a legal question to put to qualified counsel.

Does using click-to-call mean we do not need consent?

No. Manual dialing may change some regulatory questions, but Do Not Call rules, calling-time expectations and consent requirements for certain calls can still apply. The safest approach is to keep clear consent records for every number and have counsel confirm what applies to your calls. This is general information, not legal advice.

Will a power dialer make my brokers close more deals?

It can increase the number of conversations, but it does not guarantee more funded files. If brokers arrive at calls unprepared, or the list mixes very different kinds of merchants, the extra conversations may not convert. Use it for lists where every call has the same purpose, and keep complex calls on click-to-call.

How long should wrap-up time be between dialed calls?

Long enough for a broker to log the outcome and set a clear next step, often somewhere from a few seconds to under a minute depending on the call. Too short and notes get skipped, which hurts follow-up later. Let brokers pause the session when a call needs longer notes rather than forcing a fixed timer.
