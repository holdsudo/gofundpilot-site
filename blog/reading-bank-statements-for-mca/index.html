How to Read a Business Bank Statement for MCA Underwriting

Four months of PDFs land in your inbox. Here is the order to read them in, what to write down, and what an underwriter will notice before you do.

By the FundPilot teamUpdated October 15, 2026

  2. Sort the credits into revenue and everything else
  3. Read the balance, not just the flow
  4. Scan the debits for obligations
  5. Look for the pattern across months
  6. Write a one-paragraph summary a lender can trust
  7. Questions

A merchant just sent four months of business bank statements, the application is half filled in, and the merchant wants to know by this afternoon whether there is a deal. You could forward the PDFs to a funder and wait. Or you could spend fifteen minutes reading them the way an underwriter will, so you already know what the file looks like before anyone else does.

That second habit separates brokers who submit clean files from brokers who keep hearing back with questions. Reading a statement is not mysterious. It is a short sequence of checks, done in the same order every time, with notes you can stand behind when a lender asks how you got your numbers.

This guide walks through that sequence for a merchant cash advance file, where the funder is purchasing a share of future receivables and therefore cares most about how money actually flows into the account.

Pre-flight summary

  • Total deposits are a ceiling; true revenue is what remains after removing transfers, other funding proceeds and one-off credits.
  • Average and lowest daily balances show how much room the account has for a new remittance.
  • Recurring same-amount debits are the first place to look for existing positions.
  • Trends across months, and how recent any trouble is, matter more than any single figure.
  • A short, honest written summary makes your file easier to review and more credible.

Leg 01Start with the summary box, then distrust it

Every statement opens with a summary: beginning balance, total deposits and credits, total withdrawals and debits, ending balance. Write those four numbers down for each month. They give you the shape of the account in thirty seconds and a way to check your later work.

Then treat the total-deposits line as a ceiling, not an answer. That figure counts every credit, including money that has nothing to do with sales. A first-time broker who quotes total deposits as monthly revenue is setting the merchant up for an offer that shrinks at underwriting.

  • Confirm the business name and account number match the application on every page.
  • Check that statement periods are consecutive with no missing month.
  • Verify the ending balance of one month equals the beginning balance of the next.
  • Note the account type; a personal account used for business is a conversation, not a detail.

Leg 02Sort the credits into revenue and everything else

Now go line by line through the deposits. You are separating money the business earned from money that merely moved. Card processor settlements, customer payments, and checks from clients usually belong in revenue. Transfers from the owner's other accounts, proceeds from another funding provider, refunds and reversals usually do not.

Say a landscaping company shows $48,000 in total deposits for May. Reading the lines, you find a $9,000 transfer from the owner's savings account and a $3,500 equipment-sale credit that will not repeat. The underwriter will likely view true monthly revenue closer to $35,500. Better you find it now and set expectations than have the merchant hear it from a funder.

Total deposits tell you what came in. True revenue tells you what the business can keep producing.

Leg 03Read the balance, not just the flow

Average daily balance shows how much cushion the account carries. Many statements print it; if yours does not, a rough average of daily ending balances works for a first read. Two businesses with identical revenue can look very different here. One keeps $15,000 sitting in the account most days; the other dips near zero every week before payroll clears.

Look at the lowest points too. Where does the balance bottom out, and on what day of the month? A balance that consistently drops right before rent or payroll tells you the account is tight in a predictable way, which matters when a daily or weekly remittance would be added on top.

Leg 04Scan the debits for obligations

Debits tell you what the business already owes. You are looking for recurring payments that look like other funding: the same amount pulled daily or weekly, often with a descriptor that names a company or includes words like advance, capital or funding. Write down each one with its amount and frequency.

Also note large recurring obligations that are not funding but still compete for cash: rent, payroll services, tax payments, equipment leases. A funder wants to see that the business can carry its current load before taking on more.

  • Same-amount debits on a daily or weekly rhythm
  • Descriptors that change month to month for what looks like the same payee
  • Payments that stop abruptly, which may mean a payoff or a default
  • Returned items and fees attached to specific debits

Leg 05Look for the pattern across months

One month is a snapshot. Three or four months is a trend. Put your monthly revenue estimates side by side and ask whether they are steady, climbing, sliding or seasonal. A business going from $40,000 to $31,000 to $26,000 over three months tells a different story than one bouncing between $30,000 and $36,000.

Count negative-balance days and NSF or returned-item fees per month while you are at it, and note whether they cluster in the most recent month or sit further back. Recency matters more to most underwriters than raw totals.

Leg 06Write a one-paragraph summary a lender can trust

Finish by writing a short summary for the deal: estimated monthly revenue with the adjustments you made, average daily balance, NSF and negative-day counts by month, and every existing position you found. If something looks odd, say so and say what the merchant told you about it. Lenders respond well to brokers who surface issues instead of hoping they go unnoticed.

Questions from the floor

How many months of statements should I review?

Most funders ask for at least the most recent three or four months, and some want more for larger requests or seasonal businesses. Requirements vary by lender, so check each funder's guidelines. Reading more months than required is rarely wasted, because it shows whether a weak or strong month is typical or an outlier, and it lets you explain the pattern before the underwriter asks.

Can I use a merchant's online banking screenshots instead of statements?

Screenshots and transaction exports are useful for a quick early read, but most funders want official statements or a direct verification method because those are harder to alter. Treat screenshots as a preview. Ask for the full statements before you quote numbers to the merchant or submit, and note in the file which source each figure came from.

What if the merchant uses more than one business account?

Collect statements for every operating account and read them together. Transfers between the merchant's own accounts should be removed from revenue on both sides so they are not counted twice. Note which account receives card settlements and which pays the bills, since that affects where a funder would set up remittance. Disclose all accounts you know about.

Should I tell the merchant what I found before submitting?

Yes. Walking the merchant through your revenue estimate and any concerns gives them a chance to explain a large transfer or a rough month, and it sets realistic expectations about what offers might look like. It also prevents surprises later. You are not promising an outcome, just showing them how a reviewer is likely to read the account.
