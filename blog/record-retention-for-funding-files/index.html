Record Retention for Funding Files

Keep too little and you cannot answer a dispute. Keep everything forever and every old file becomes a liability. A retention policy finds the line on purpose.

Open the shared drive at most funding shops that have been around a few years and scroll to the bottom. You will find folders for deals that died before an offer was ever made, holding complete applications, identity documents and six months of statements. Nobody has looked at them in years. Nobody has deleted them either.

Meanwhile, ask the same shop to produce the signed contract and disclosure for a deal funded eighteen months ago, and it may take an afternoon of searching inboxes. That is the retention problem in a nutshell: the wrong things kept, the right things hard to find.

This guide lays out a practical way to decide what to keep, how to set retention periods with professional advice, and how to delete properly. It does not tell you specific legal timeframes. Those depend on your jurisdiction, your business model and your contracts, and they need to come from qualified counsel.

Pre-flight summary

  • Group records into categories so each can carry its own retention rule.
  • Get retention periods, legal hold guidance and deletion requirements from qualified counsel.
  • Keeping everything forever adds exposure, especially for declined applications.
  • Store records on the deal so you can retrieve them quickly when needed.
  • Delete on a schedule and account for every copy, including email and backups.

Leg 01Sort your records into categories first

Retention is easier when you stop thinking about files and start thinking about categories. Each category can then carry its own rule. Most funding shops have some version of these.

  • Funded deal records: applications, statements used in underwriting, offers, disclosures, signed contracts and funding confirmations.
  • Declined or abandoned applications, which often contain the same sensitive data with no ongoing business purpose.
  • Communications: emails, texts, call recordings and transcripts tied to deals.
  • Financial records: commissions, syndication participations, payouts and payoffs.
  • Marketing data: leads, opt-outs and consent records for calling and texting.
  • System records: audit logs, user access history and permission changes.

Leg 02Questions to settle with counsel

With categories in hand, the legal conversation becomes specific. Rather than asking how long you should keep everything, you ask about each category in turn. Several sources can drive the answer: laws that apply to your business and location, requirements in your agreements with funders and partners, tax and accounting needs, and the practical window during which disputes might arise.

Ask your lawyer to identify any category where a minimum retention period applies, any where keeping data longer than needed creates risk, and what to do with records connected to a dispute or investigation. That last point matters: when litigation is reasonably anticipated, normal deletion may need to pause for the affected records, a concept often called a legal hold.

Data you no longer hold cannot be leaked, subpoenaed by surprise or misused by someone who has left.

Leg 03Why keeping everything forever is not the safe option

Keeping every file can feel cautious. It is not. Every stored application is something that can be exposed in a breach, accessed by a departing broker or swept into a broad discovery request. Old opt-out lists you deleted too early are a problem; old identity documents you kept for no reason are a different problem.

Consider a hypothetical shop that receives hundreds of applications a month and funds a fraction of them. If it never deletes declined files, within a few years the overwhelming majority of the sensitive data it holds belongs to merchants it never did business with. That is pure exposure with no business value.

Leg 04Retrieval is half of retention

A record you keep but cannot find does not help you. Retention works best when records for a deal live together in one place, attached to that deal, rather than scattered across inboxes and drives.

In FundPilot, documents, calls, texts, lender replies and offers attach to the deal, and file storage keeps version history, so you can see which version of a contract existed at a given time. When a question comes up about a specific merchant, you open one record instead of searching five systems.

Leg 05Deleting securely and completely

Deletion is where good policies quietly fail. A file removed from the CRM view may still exist in an email attachment, a download folder, a backup or a broker's phone. A real deletion process accounts for every place a record might live.

  1. Write down where each record category is stored, including email, file storage, phones and backups.
  2. Agree retention periods per category with counsel and document them.
  3. Set a recurring calendar date, monthly or quarterly, to identify records past their period.
  4. Check for legal holds before deleting anything.
  5. Delete from the primary system and confirm how backups age out.
  6. Record what was deleted and when, without retaining the deleted content itself.
  7. Reduce future copies by collecting documents through a portal and keeping them on the deal.

Leg 06Making it stick

Retention policies usually die from neglect, not disagreement. Assign one owner, keep the policy short enough to read in five minutes, and tie it to a recurring date rather than good intentions. Review it with counsel whenever you add a product, enter new states or change systems.

The audit trail helps here too. FundPilot logs exports, downloads and changes, so you can show that the policy is followed, not just written down.

Questions from the floor

How long should we keep funded deal files?

There is no single answer that fits every shop. Retention periods can be shaped by laws in your jurisdiction, your agreements with funders, tax and accounting needs and the realistic window for disputes. Bring your record categories to qualified counsel and get a written retention schedule. Then apply it consistently, because a sensible policy that is followed beats a perfect one that is ignored.

Should we keep declined applications?

Declined and abandoned applications often hold the same sensitive data as funded files with far less reason to keep it. Some records, such as consent or opt-out history, may need to be kept regardless. Ask counsel which parts of a declined file you should retain and for how long, then delete the rest on schedule rather than letting it pile up indefinitely.

What is a legal hold?

A legal hold generally means pausing normal deletion for records relevant to a dispute, investigation or anticipated litigation, so they are preserved. Your counsel will tell you when one applies and which records it covers. Build a step into your deletion routine that checks for active holds before anything is removed, and make sure whoever runs deletions knows how to recognize one.

Do opt-out and consent records follow the same schedule as deal files?

Often they need different treatment. Records showing that someone asked not to be called or texted, or gave consent to be contacted, can matter long after a deal file has no other purpose. Treat them as their own category in your policy and ask qualified counsel how long to keep them. Deleting them too early can create a different kind of risk.
