Replacing Five Tools With One: The Hidden Cost of a Scattered Stack

A dialer here, a spreadsheet there, statements in email and commissions in a notebook. Each tool looks cheap on its own. Together they quietly tax every deal.

Follow a single deal through a typical funding shop and count the windows. The lead arrives in one place. The broker dials from a separate dialer, scribbles notes, then retypes them into the CRM. Statements come in by email and get saved to a shared drive. Someone reviews them in a spreadsheet. Submissions go out by email again, lender replies land in three different inboxes, and the commission ends up in another spreadsheet that only the owner opens.

None of those tools is bad. Most were added for a good reason at the time. The trouble is what happens in the gaps between them, where information is copied by hand, delayed, or simply never arrives.

This guide is about putting a rough number on that cost in your own shop and deciding, calmly, which pieces belong together.

Pre-flight summary

  • The real cost of a scattered stack is switching, retyping and missing data, not licence fees.
  • A one-week audit turns a vague complaint into a number your team agrees on.
  • Data gaps are the most dangerous cost because they are invisible until a deal stalls.
  • Anything about a specific merchant or deal belongs on the deal record.
  • Share the audit with brokers so the change is about their time.

Leg 01Three costs that never show up on an invoice

The subscription fees for a scattered stack are visible. The larger costs are not, and they tend to fall into three buckets.

  • Context switching: every jump between tools breaks a broker's focus and adds seconds that compound over hundreds of dials and dozens of files a week.
  • Double entry: the same merchant name, phone number and revenue figure typed into three systems, each time with a chance of a typo.
  • Data gaps: the call that was never logged, the lender reply sitting in a personal inbox, the commission note that only one person understands.

Leg 02Run a one-week stack audit

You do not need consultants to see the problem. Pick one week and have two or three brokers keep a simple tally. It is tedious, but the results usually settle the argument.

Say a broker reports re-entering merchant details an average of four times per file, and spends a few minutes per lender reply just finding it and copying the gist into the deal. Multiply by your weekly file count and the hidden labour becomes concrete. The point is not precision. The point is replacing a vague sense of friction with a number your team agrees on.

  1. List every tool touched between first contact and funded commission.
  2. For five real deals, count how many times core merchant data was typed by hand.
  3. Note every time a broker had to ask a colleague where something was.
  4. Record which activities were never logged anywhere, such as calls from a personal phone.
  5. Ask a manager to answer three pipeline questions using only the CRM, and note what they had to look up elsewhere.

Leg 03Where the gaps actually hurt

Data gaps are the most dangerous of the three costs because they look like nothing at all. A deal stalls and the manager cannot tell whether the broker called the merchant twice yesterday or not since last week, because the dialer and the CRM do not talk to each other.

Gaps also hurt at renewal time. If the funded amount, the lender and the payoff schedule live in a separate ledger, nobody gets prompted when a merchant is far enough paid down to discuss a renewal. And they hurt when a merchant asks a simple question, like which documents they still owe, and the honest answer is that someone needs to check their email.

Finally, there is exposure. Every additional tool holding merchant bank statements is another place to secure, another set of logins and another export nobody is tracking.

The most expensive data in a funding shop is the data that was never written down anywhere.

Leg 04What consolidation looks like in practice

Consolidating does not mean throwing everything away. It means pulling the steps of a single deal into one record so nothing has to be copied. In FundPilot, for example, the dialer is built in: browser calling, texting and the power dialer all log each call to the deal, and recordings become searchable transcripts with summaries attached to the same file.

The same idea extends down the deal. Statement underwriting sits on the merchant record with each figure linked to the source page. Lender submissions go out in batches and replies thread back onto the deal. Funded deals feed a single ledger covering participations, payoff schedules and broker payouts. Documents, spreadsheets and team chat live inside the CRM rather than in a separate drive.

When the pieces share one record, the manager's three pipeline questions get answered in one place.

Leg 05When keeping a separate tool still makes sense

Be honest about the exceptions. If your accounting team relies on dedicated accounting software for the company books, that is a different job from tracking broker commissions on deals, and it may well stay separate. If a particular lead source arrives through a feed you cannot change, the goal is to have it land in the CRM automatically rather than to replace it.

A useful test: does this tool hold information about a specific merchant or deal? If yes, it probably belongs on the deal record. If it serves the business as a whole, consolidation is less urgent.

Leg 06Making the switch without a revolt

Brokers resist new tools for understandable reasons. They have muscle memory, and a slow week costs them money. Share the audit results with the floor so the case is about their time, not management's preference. Move one workflow at a time if you can, starting with the one that causes the most double entry. Give new and nervous brokers a practice mode to try the system before it counts, and make sure live data is migrated properly so nobody has to rebuild their pipeline by hand.

If you want to see what a single deal record looks like when the dialer, underwriting, lender replies and commissions all share it, a FundPilot walkthrough using one of your own recent files is a reasonable place to start.

Questions from the floor

Is an all-in-one platform always better than best-of-breed tools?

Not automatically. A single platform is better when the pieces genuinely share data, so a call, a statement and a lender reply all land on the same deal. If an all-in-one product just bundles separate modules that still need manual copying, you have the same gaps under one login. Judge it by whether a deal's full history is visible in one place.

How do I convince a top broker who loves their current dialer?

Start with what they care about: time on the phone and deals closed. Show them the audit numbers for their own week, then let them test the new dialer on real calls before anything is switched off. Top producers usually accept change once they see it saves them re-entry time rather than adding steps.

Does consolidating tools improve security?

It can, because there are fewer places holding merchant data and fewer logins to manage. It helps most when the platform offers an isolated workspace per company, two-factor sign-in, role-based access and an audit trail of exports and downloads. Fewer tools is not a security plan by itself, but it makes one much easier to enforce.
