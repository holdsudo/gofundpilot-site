Underwriting Seasonal Businesses Fairly

A pool contractor in February and a pool contractor in July are the same business. Your file has to make that obvious to someone who only sees four months.

A merchant who runs a snow-removal company calls you in late March. Their last four statements show December through March: strong, busy months. Next month, their deposits will collapse until November. If you submit those four statements without a word of context, the funder sees a business doing very well. If you submit them in August, the funder sees a business nearly asleep. Neither picture is true.

Seasonal businesses are everywhere: landscapers, tax preparers, ice cream shops, beach rentals, holiday retailers, HVAC contractors, wedding venues. They are often healthy, long-running companies. They simply earn their year unevenly. Underwriting them fairly means looking at the whole cycle and making sure any funding fits the slow months as well as the busy ones.

Pre-flight summary

  • Three or four months of statements can badly mislead for a seasonal business.
  • Request a longer history or prior-year months to see the whole cycle.
  • Size any funding against the slow months, not the peak.
  • Present the seasonal pattern clearly so the funder does not have to reconstruct it.
  • Talk to the merchant honestly about carrying payments through the off-season.

Leg 01Why a short statement window misleads

Most files start with three or four months of statements. For a business with stable revenue, that is a reasonable sample. For a seasonal one, it captures only part of the cycle, and the part depends entirely on when the merchant happened to apply.

Applying in peak season inflates the picture, and a remittance sized on peak deposits can become unmanageable when revenue drops. Applying in the off-season understates the business, and a perfectly sound company may be declined or offered far less than it could carry. Both outcomes are unfair, in different directions.

Four months of a seasonal business is a chapter, not the story.

Leg 02Ask for more of the year

Where a business is clearly seasonal, request a longer history, ideally twelve months of statements, or prior-year statements for the same months you are looking at. If the merchant has been in business several years, a year-over-year comparison of the same season is especially useful: it separates normal seasonal swings from genuine decline.

Explain the request to the merchant as being in their interest. If they applied in the off-season, the extra months show the strength they cannot see in the recent statements. If they applied at peak, the extra months protect them from taking on payments sized for July that they will struggle with in January.

Leg 03Map the cycle month by month

Lay out true revenue for every month you have, after removing transfers and other non-revenue credits. Then look at the shape. When does the business ramp up? When is peak? How deep and how long is the trough? How does the account balance behave in the slow months: does the owner build reserves at peak and draw them down, or does the balance hit zero?

Take a hypothetical landscaping company: true revenue around $70,000 a month from May through September, $35,000 in April and October, and roughly $9,000 in the winter months. Annual revenue near $490,000, monthly average around $41,000. A remittance sized on $70,000 summer months would be impossible to carry in January.

  1. Calculate true revenue for each month available.
  2. Identify peak, shoulder and off-season months.
  3. Calculate annual total and true monthly average.
  4. Note the lowest month's revenue and lowest balances.
  5. Check year-over-year for the same months if history allows.
  6. Record where in the cycle the merchant is applying.

Leg 04Presenting seasonality to lenders

Lenders handle seasonal businesses in different ways, and some are better suited to them than others. Your job is to make the pattern obvious so the funder does not have to reconstruct it.

Open your broker summary with a sentence that names the seasonality directly, followed by the monthly figures and how the business manages the slow period. If the owner builds a cash reserve, show it. If the business has a stable off-season income, such as snow removal for a landscaper, show that too. Point out where in the cycle the application falls.

  • A one-line description of the seasonal pattern
  • Monthly true revenue across the full cycle available
  • Year-over-year comparison of the same season, if available
  • How the business covers the off-season, with evidence
  • Where the current statements fall in the cycle

Leg 05Funding that fits the slow months

Even a well-presented seasonal file carries a real risk for the merchant: a fixed daily or weekly remittance does not shrink when revenue does. Talk to the merchant about the off-season before they accept anything. What will the payment be as a share of their slowest month? Do they have reserves to cover the gap?

Some funders offer structures that better suit uneven revenue, and timing matters too. Funding just before the busy season is very different from funding just before the trough. Present the options honestly and help the merchant pick the one they can sustain all year, not just the one with the largest number.

A simple test helps. Take the proposed monthly remittance and set it against the slowest month's true revenue and the balance the business typically carries into that month. If the payment would consume most of what comes in during the trough, the amount is probably too large for this merchant right now, however good the summer looks.

Leg 06Keeping the context with the deal

Seasonal files are where context gets lost most easily. The broker who understood the cycle leaves, or the renewal comes up eleven months later and nobody remembers why January looked so thin.

Questions from the floor

How do I know if a business is seasonal?

Ask the merchant, and look at the industry. Then check the statements: a steady climb or drop across consecutive months, or a sharp change in deposits at a predictable time of year, are strong hints. A year of statements, or prior-year statements for the same months, settles it. Some businesses are mildly seasonal, with a modest swing, while others nearly stop for part of the year.

Should I wait for peak season to submit a seasonal merchant?

Do not time a submission to make the business look stronger than it is. Present the full cycle whenever the merchant applies. The right timing is when the merchant actually needs funds and can carry the payments through the next slow period. Submitting peak months alone, without context, risks an offer the merchant cannot sustain once revenue drops.

Do all funders accept seasonal businesses?

No. Some are cautious about seasonal revenue, while others are comfortable with it when the pattern is well documented and stable. Guidelines change, so check each lender's current criteria rather than relying on past experience. Clear presentation of the full cycle gives every funder the best chance to assess the file fairly.
