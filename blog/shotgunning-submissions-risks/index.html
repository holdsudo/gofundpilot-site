Why Shotgunning Submissions Hurts Brokers and Merchants

Blasting a file to twenty funders looks like hustle. In practice it burns the merchant's credit, the funders' patience and your shop's reputation for very little extra.

Every floor has a broker who does it. The application comes in, the statements land, and within ten minutes the file is in twenty inboxes. The logic sounds airtight: more submissions, more chances, faster offer. When a deal funds, the broker remembers the speed. When it does not, nobody connects the outcome to the method.

Shotgunning, sending a file to as many funders as possible without matching it to their guidelines, carries costs that land on three parties at once. The merchant absorbs some, the funders absorb some, and your shop absorbs the rest in ways that only show up months later. Understanding those costs is the first step to replacing the habit with something that works better.

Pre-flight summary

  • Shotgunning can add credit inquiries and spreads sensitive merchant data more widely than needed.
  • Funders track broker submission quality and treat careless shops accordingly.
  • Mass submissions create conflicting offers, ownership disputes and stacking temptations.
  • Targeted rounds, matched to guidelines, can be just as fast and far more credible.
  • A written, visible submission policy changes floor habits faster than individual coaching.

Leg 01What the merchant pays for

Many funders check the business owner's credit as part of reviewing a file. Depending on the funder and how they pull, a burst of checks in a short window may show up on the owner's report. The merchant asked for one funding option and ended up with a trail of inquiries they did not expect and may not understand.

There is also the privacy cost. A funding file contains bank statements, tax identification, ownership details and often a driver's license. Every extra submission puts that information in another company's systems. A merchant who agreed to let you shop their file reasonably expects you to send it where it has a real chance, not everywhere at once.

Finally, a scattered approach often produces a scattered experience. The merchant starts receiving calls and emails from funders they never heard of, sometimes before you have even spoken to them about an offer.

Leg 02What the funders notice

Underwriters see the same file arrive from several brokers, or see the same merchant appear on a shared database inquiry within hours of their own review. They learn quickly which shops match files carefully and which ones send everything. Files from the second group get looked at later, with more suspicion, and with less willingness to stretch on a borderline case.

Funders also track their own submission-to-funded ratio by broker. A shop that sends a large volume of files outside a funder's guidelines produces a poor ratio, and that number shapes how the relationship is treated. Some funders limit or end relationships with brokers whose submissions consistently miss.

Underwriters learn which shops match files carefully and which ones send everything, and they read the second group's files last.

Leg 03Conflicting offers and who owns the deal

When a file goes everywhere, several offers can come back at once with different amounts, terms and conditions. That sounds like a good problem until you try to explain six offers to a merchant, three of which came from funders that will change terms once they see the full picture.

Shotgunning also creates ownership disputes. If another broker has already submitted the same merchant to a funder, the funder may treat the earlier submission as the one that counts. Your shop can do the work on a file and still lose the deal on timing, simply because the file went to a funder where it was already sitting.

And there is a stacking risk. A merchant who receives several approvals at once may be tempted to accept more than one. That outcome rarely ends well for the business, and a responsible broker should be steering away from it, not making it easier.

Leg 04What your shop loses over time

The short-term cost of shotgunning is invisible because the occasional fast funding hides it. The long-term cost shows up as slower responses from funders, fewer exceptions on borderline files and a reputation that follows your shop's name into every underwriting queue.

Internally, shotgunning hides skill gaps. A broker who sends everywhere never has to learn which funder fits which file, so they never get better at it. When that broker moves on, the shop has learned nothing about its own lender panel from the files they handled.

  • Slower turnaround from funders who have learned to deprioritize your files
  • Fewer exceptions on near-miss files, because there is no trust to draw on
  • Lost deals to ownership timing conflicts
  • Merchant complaints about calls from funders they never chose
  • No reliable data about which funders actually suit which files

Leg 05A targeted alternative that is still fast

Targeted submission does not mean slow submission. It means sending to a short, reasoned list in a planned order. Most of the time saved by shotgunning is time that should have been spent matching, and matching can be quick when guidelines are stored properly.

FundPilot's lender matching scores the file against each funder's guidelines, then lets a broker send a batch submission to the funders they choose, with replies threaded back onto the deal. The broker still sends several submissions at once when that is appropriate; the difference is that each one has a reason.

  1. Tell the merchant how many funders you plan to approach and get their agreement.
  2. Apply knockout criteria and drop any funder the file cannot fit.
  3. Rank the rest by fit and start with the lowest-cost tier the file qualifies for.
  4. Send a small first round, typically a handful, rather than the whole panel.
  5. Wait for responses within a set window before widening the round.
  6. Record why each funder was chosen so the reasoning can be reviewed later.

Leg 06Setting a floor-wide rule

Habits like shotgunning are rarely fixed one broker at a time. Set a written submission policy for the floor: how many funders a first round can include, who approves a wider round, and what has to be on the file before anything is sent.

Then make the policy visible. When submissions, replies and outcomes are tracked on each deal in FundPilot, a manager can see how many funders each file went to and how those submissions performed. That turns the conversation with a broker from an opinion into a shared look at the results.

Questions from the floor

How many funders should a file go to in the first round?

There is no universal number, and it depends on the file and your panel. Many shops settle on a small first round, often a handful of well-matched funders, then widen only if those do not respond with something workable. The key is that every funder in the round was chosen because the file fits their guidelines, and that the merchant knows roughly how many submissions to expect.

Do all funders pull credit on submission?

Practices vary by funder. Some review credit early, some only after an initial look at statements, and how a check appears on the owner's report depends on how it is pulled. Because you cannot always control this, the safest approach is to assume each submission may involve a credit check, tell the merchant so, and limit submissions to funders with a genuine chance of making an offer.

Is shotgunning ever justified?

It is hard to justify sending a file to funders that cannot take it. There are cases where a wider round makes sense, such as a strong file where several funders are a genuine fit and the merchant wants to compare. That is still a targeted round, just a larger one. The problem is not the number on its own; it is sending without matching and without the merchant's informed agreement.
