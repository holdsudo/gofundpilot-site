Speed to Lead: Why the First Call Decides the Deal

A merchant who fills out a funding form is usually filling out three. The shop that reaches them first, with something useful to say, sets the terms of the conversation.

It is 10:42 on a Tuesday. A restaurant owner who needs to replace a walk-in cooler submits a form on your site. By 10:44 she has also submitted forms on two other sites, because that is what people do when they are worried about cash. Her phone is in her hand. Whoever calls in the next few minutes is the one she talks to while the problem is still front of mind.

Meanwhile, on a lot of floors, that lead sits in a shared inbox until someone notices it after lunch. Nobody did anything wrong, exactly. There was just no rule for whose lead it was, no alert that reached a person, and no plan for what happens if the assigned broker is already on a call.

Speed to lead is not about being frantic. It is about building a short, boring chain of steps so that every inbound inquiry reaches a qualified human quickly and gets a real conversation, not just a fast one.

Pre-flight summary

  • Most delay lives between the lead existing and a named person knowing it is theirs.
  • Every routing rule needs a timed fallback so leads do not stall with an absent owner.
  • Alerts should reach the device a broker carries, with enough detail to call immediately.
  • After-hours leads get a permitted acknowledgment and a first-in-queue morning call.
  • Track response time per broker and per source, then compare against deals that progress.

Leg 01Map the minutes between form and first ring

Before changing anything, trace what actually happens today. Submit a test inquiry yourself at a few different times of day and write down each handoff: when the record is created, when someone is told, when a dial happens, and when a human says hello. Most shops find the delay is not in dialing at all. It is in the gap between the lead existing and a specific person knowing it is theirs.

Look for the silent stalls. A form that emails a group address. A lead that lands unassigned because the source was not mapped. A broker who gets the alert on a desktop they are not sitting at. Each of these adds minutes that nobody sees on a report.

  • Record created: does every source land in the CRM automatically, or does someone paste it in?
  • Owner assigned: is it instant, or does a manager hand leads out in batches?
  • Owner notified: on the device they actually carry, with enough detail to call?
  • First attempt logged: can you see the time of the first dial against the time of the form?

Leg 02Routing rules that decide ownership instantly

The fastest routing rule is the one nobody has to think about. Pick a primary method and write it down. Round robin works well when brokers are roughly equal in experience. Weighted distribution makes sense when you want senior people to get a larger share of larger requests. Source-based routing helps when a referral partner expects to deal with the same broker every time.

Whatever you choose, build in a fallback. If the assigned broker does not make an attempt within a window you set, the lead should move to the next available person and the original owner should be told why. Without that rule, round robin just distributes delay evenly.

Be careful with fairness. Brokers notice quickly if the morning leads always go to one desk. Rotating the starting point daily and skipping people who are marked away keeps the system trusted, which matters more than squeezing out an extra few seconds.

Leg 03Alerts that reach a person, not an inbox

An alert only counts if it interrupts someone who can act on it. For most floors that means a push notification on the phone and a visible flag in the dialer queue, with the merchant name, business type, requested amount and lead source in the preview. The broker should be able to decide in a glance whether to drop what they are doing.

In FundPilot, new inbound records can drop straight into a broker's power dialer queue, and because the dialer runs in the browser and on iPhone and iPad, the alert and the call button live in the same place. That removes the step where someone reads an email, opens the CRM, finds the record and then picks up a desk phone.

Routing that spreads leads fairly but slowly is just distributing delay. Every rule needs a fallback with a clock on it.

Leg 04What to say in the first ninety seconds

Calling fast and then fumbling wastes the head start. The opening should confirm who you are, why you are calling and that you saw what they asked for. Something like: you just reached out about funding for equipment, I have your request in front of me, do you have two minutes so I can understand what you need and tell you honestly whether we can help.

That last part matters. A merchant who has just submitted several forms is bracing for pressure. Being the calm, specific caller who asks about the business before talking about offers is how you become the one they call back. Avoid promising amounts, timelines or approvals on the first call; you have not seen a statement yet.

If you reach voicemail, leave a short message with your name, company and a direct callback number, then follow with a text where the merchant has given permission to be texted. The text should say the same thing in fewer words.

Leg 05After-hours and weekend inquiries

Plenty of business owners only get to paperwork after closing. A form at 9:30 at night is not a lead to call at 9:31. Calling outside reasonable local hours can annoy people and may raise compliance questions about calling-time restrictions, so set your rules with counsel and let the system enforce them.

Instead, send an immediate, permitted acknowledgment that a person will call in the morning, and queue the lead so it is the first thing the assigned broker sees when the floor opens. FundPilot campaigns can respect the merchant's local time and stop automatically on reply, which keeps an overnight inquiry from turning into a stack of messages they did not want.

  1. Capture the lead and assign an owner even when nobody is on shift.
  2. Send one short acknowledgment through a channel the merchant agreed to.
  3. Hold calls until your approved local calling window opens.
  4. Put overnight leads at the top of the owner's morning queue.
  5. Escalate any lead still untouched an hour after opening.

Leg 06Measure response time per broker, not per floor

A floor average hides the problem. Say your average first-attempt time looks fine, but one broker takes most of the leads in under five minutes while two others routinely take over an hour. The average blends them into something that looks acceptable and fixes nothing.

Report time-to-first-attempt and time-to-first-conversation by broker, by lead source and by hour of day. Then compare those against which deals actually moved to submission. You are looking for patterns in your own numbers, not an industry benchmark. Because FundPilot logs every call to the deal record, those timestamps are already there; the work is in reviewing them weekly and adjusting routing.

Questions from the floor

How fast should a broker call a new inbound business funding lead?

As soon as practical within your permitted calling hours, and fast enough that the merchant still remembers submitting the form. Rather than chasing a universal number, measure your own time-to-first-attempt against which leads progress to a submitted file. If quicker attempts correlate with more conversations on your floor, tighten your fallback window until you see the curve flatten.

Is round robin the best way to distribute leads?

It is a sensible default when brokers are similar in experience, because it is simple and feels fair. Many shops blend it with exceptions: referral partners routed to a dedicated broker, larger requests weighted toward senior people, and absent brokers skipped automatically. The method matters less than having a written rule and a fallback that moves untouched leads.

Should we call leads that come in late at night?

Generally no. Send a brief acknowledgment through a channel the merchant has agreed to, then call when your approved local calling window opens. Calling-time restrictions and consent requirements vary, so confirm your rules with qualified counsel and let your system enforce them. This is general information, not legal advice.

What if the assigned broker is on another call?

That is exactly what the fallback rule is for. Set a window, and if no attempt is logged within it, reassign the lead to the next available broker and notify both people. Brokers should also be able to mark themselves away so the round robin skips them during long calls, training or lunch.
