Stacking, Explained Honestly for Brokers

Stacking can solve a short-term cash problem or create a much bigger one. A broker's job is to know which, and to say so plainly.

Every funding floor hears the same request sooner or later. A merchant already has an advance, the daily debit is biting, and they want more. Maybe a supplier is demanding payment, maybe a slow month caught them off guard. They are asking you to find a second position, or a third.

This is stacking, and it is one of the most consequential decisions in the business. Done carefully, with full disclosure and a clear-eyed look at cash flow, an additional position can sometimes bridge a real gap. Done carelessly, it can push a business into a cycle where each new advance mostly services the last one. Brokers who understand the difference, and act on it, build longer careers and better lender relationships than those who do not.

Pre-flight summary

  • Stacking means multiple advances remitting from the same account at the same time.
  • Each added position compounds the daily burden and is usually more expensive than the last.
  • Funder tolerance varies widely, and existing agreements may restrict new financing.
  • Disclose every position and calculate the combined burden against true revenue.
  • Sometimes the most responsible answer, and the best long-term one, is to wait.

Leg 01What stacking actually means

Stacking is when a merchant takes a new merchant cash advance while one or more existing advances are still being remitted. Each funder has purchased a portion of the merchant's future receivables, and each takes its remittance from the same account. The new position is often called a second, third or later position, in order of when it was funded.

Stacking is different from a renewal with the same funder, where the existing balance is typically settled from the new funding, and from a consolidation, where a new funder pays off one or more existing positions. In a true stack, all the positions keep running at the same time.

Leg 02The risk to the merchant, in plain numbers

Consider a hypothetical café with true monthly revenue around $45,000. Its first position takes about $250 per business day, roughly $5,500 a month. Tight but workable. A second position adds another $300 per day. Now around $12,000 a month, more than a quarter of revenue, leaves the account before rent, payroll, suppliers or the owner's pay.

Each additional position also usually carries a higher cost than the last, because each new funder is behind the others and takes more risk. A third or fourth position can mean that a large share of every sale is already committed. If revenue dips even modestly, payments start to bounce, fees pile up, and the merchant faces a crisis that a smaller, slower decision might have avoided.

The question is never whether you can find a funder. It is whether the business can carry what you find.

Leg 03How funders tend to view it

Funders vary widely. Some will only fund first positions. Some will consider a second position when cash flow clearly supports it. Some specialise in later positions and price accordingly. Many funding agreements include terms restricting the merchant from taking additional financing without consent, and existing funders may react if they see a new position appear.

Because of all that, never assume. Read each lender's current guidelines and agreements, and send files only to funders whose criteria the stack genuinely fits. Leg 04A responsible approach, step by step

You do not need a policy manual to handle stacking requests well. You need a consistent routine that puts the merchant's ability to carry the payments ahead of the commission.

  1. Identify every existing position from the statements and confirm each with the merchant.
  2. Calculate the total current remittance as a share of true monthly revenue.
  3. Add the proposed new remittance and look at the combined share honestly.
  4. Ask what the funds are for and whether that need would actually be solved by the amount.
  5. Explain to the merchant, in writing, the combined daily or weekly payments they would be carrying.
  6. Encourage the merchant to review their existing agreements, and to seek independent advice if unsure.
  7. Submit only to funders whose guidelines fit, with every position disclosed.

Leg 05When the right answer is no, or not yet

Sometimes the numbers say the business cannot safely carry another position. In that case the most valuable thing you can do is tell the merchant. Explain what you see, show them the combined burden, and talk through alternatives: waiting until an existing position finishes, asking whether a consolidation offer would genuinely lower their payments, or addressing the underlying cash problem another way.

Merchants remember who was straight with them. A broker who turned down a risky stack in spring is often the first call in autumn when the first position is paid off and the business is ready for a sensible renewal.

Leg 06Keeping the record straight

Write down what you advised and why. Note every position you found, the combined burden you calculated, and what the merchant said when you discussed it. If a dispute ever arises, a clear record of disclosure and advice protects everyone involved.

FundPilot keeps statement analysis, call transcripts, notes and lender replies together on the deal, with a full audit trail of changes, so that record builds itself as you work rather than relying on memory. Managers can review stacking deals before they go out, which is a sensible control for any floor that wants these decisions made carefully.

Questions from the floor

Is stacking illegal?

Stacking is not inherently unlawful, but it raises contractual and disclosure issues. Many funding agreements restrict additional financing, and misrepresenting existing positions can expose a merchant to serious consequences. Rules on commercial financing disclosures and broker conduct also vary by jurisdiction and change over time. This is general information, not legal advice; confirm current requirements with qualified counsel for your situation.

Should a broker ever recommend a second position?

A second position can sometimes make sense when cash flow comfortably supports the combined payments, the purpose is clear and productive, and everything is disclosed. The decision belongs to the merchant, informed by honest numbers from you. Your role is to show them the full picture, including the combined payment burden and cost, and to avoid pressuring them toward funding that strains the business.

How is a consolidation different from stacking?

In a consolidation, a new funder pays off one or more existing positions as part of the new funding, so the merchant ends up with fewer simultaneous remittances. In stacking, all positions keep running. A consolidation is not automatically better; compare the total cost and the new payment against what the merchant pays today. Lay both options side by side before the merchant decides.
