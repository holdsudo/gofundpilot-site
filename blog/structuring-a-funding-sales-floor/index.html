How to Structure a Business-Funding Sales Floor

Openers and closers, full-cycle brokers, or pods: how to pick the floor model that fits your headcount, your lead flow and the deals you actually fund.

Most funding shops never choose a floor structure. They inherit one. The founder ran full-cycle deals alone, hired two people who did the same, and then one day there are nine brokers, a shared lead pool, arguments over who owns a renewal, and nobody can say why a merchant who submitted an application on Tuesday still has no offer on Friday.

Structure is the answer to a small set of questions: who makes the first contact, who collects the file, who talks to lenders, who presents the offer, and who owns the merchant after funding. There are only a few workable ways to split those jobs, and each one fits a different stage of a shop's growth. This guide walks through the three common models, the hybrid that many mid-sized floors land on, and the handoff rules that make any of them work.

Pre-flight summary

  • List the jobs a deal requires before you assign titles.
  • Full-cycle suits small teams; openers and closers suit heavy purchased-lead volume.
  • Pods combine specialisation with shared ownership and scale by addition.
  • Every handoff needs a written standard and a time limit.
  • Record ownership and commission splits on the deal, not in memory.

Leg 01Start with the jobs, not the titles

Before you draw an org chart, list the work a deal requires from first ring to funded. On a typical business-funding floor that list looks something like this, and every structure is just a decision about which person does which line.

Write down, honestly, how many hours a week each line takes in your shop today. If statement collection eats most of the week, a structure that leaves it to your best talker will cost you deals no matter what you call the role.

  • Prospecting: dialing, texting and working inbound leads until a merchant engages
  • Qualification: time in business, monthly deposits, industry, existing positions, use of funds
  • File building: application, bank statements, ID, any supporting documents
  • Submission: choosing lenders, packaging the file, chasing replies
  • Presentation: walking the merchant through offers in plain terms
  • Contracts to funding: stipulations, verification calls, closing
  • Account management: renewals, check-ins, referrals

Leg 02Model one: full-cycle brokers

In a full-cycle shop, each broker owns a merchant from the first call to the renewal. This is how almost every floor starts, and for a team of one to roughly five people it is usually the right answer. The merchant hears one voice, there are no handoffs to fumble, and ownership disputes are rare because the broker who opened the lead keeps it.

The weakness shows up as volume grows. A broker who is chasing stipulations on three deals is not dialing, so new-lead contact slows down exactly when the pipeline is fullest. You also end up with uneven skills: someone excellent on the phone may be careless with files, and lenders notice incomplete submissions.

Full-cycle works best when lead flow is modest, when your brokers are experienced, and when you support them with tools that remove the administrative drag, such as statement analysis and lender submission tracking, so their selling time is protected.

Leg 03Model two: openers and closers

The opener-closer split is the classic high-volume floor. Openers live on the dialer, qualify fast and get an application started. Once a merchant meets the basics, the deal transfers to a closer who builds the file, works lenders and presents the offer.

Say your shop buys a few hundred fresh leads a week. A single full-cycle broker cannot call them all within minutes, but three openers on a power dialer can, and two closers can absorb the qualified handful each opener produces. Specialisation lets each person get very good at one conversation.

The cost is the handoff. Merchants dislike repeating their story, and openers paid on transfers have an incentive to pass along weak deals. If you run this model, define a transfer standard in writing: what must be confirmed, what must be in the file, and what the closer will send back.

A handoff is only as good as the notes that travel with it. If the closer has to re-ask, the transfer did not happen.

Leg 04Model three: pods

A pod is a small team that runs complete deals together: commonly one or two openers, one closer and a processor who handles file building and submissions. Each pod owns a slice of the lead flow, a vertical, or a set of lender relationships, and is measured as a unit.

Pods give you the efficiency of specialisation with a shared sense of ownership. The opener knows the closer personally, the processor knows which lenders each closer prefers, and coaching happens inside the group. They also make growth simpler: when you hire, you add a pod rather than reshuffling the whole floor.

Pods need a manager who watches for imbalance. One pod sitting on a full pipeline while another starves for leads is a routing problem, and it should be fixed by lead distribution rather than by poaching deals across teams.

Leg 05Matching the model to your size

There is no correct structure in the abstract, only one that fits your current headcount and lead volume. Use this as a rough starting point and adjust to what your numbers tell you.

  • One to five brokers with mostly referral or renewal flow: full-cycle, with shared processing support if you can afford it
  • Five to twelve brokers with heavy purchased-lead volume: openers and closers, with a written transfer standard
  • Twelve or more brokers, or several lead sources and verticals: pods, each with its own processor
  • Any size with a big renewal book: a dedicated account manager who owns funded merchants
  • New shops still finding product-market fit: stay full-cycle until the bottleneck is obvious

Leg 06Make the structure visible in your CRM

Whatever you choose, encode it. Ownership fields should show who opened the merchant and who owns the deal now, so commissions and renewals are never decided by memory. Pipeline stages should mark the handoff points, so a deal sitting at qualified with no closer assigned is obvious at a glance.

Commission rules belong in the same place. If openers earn on transfers and closers on fundings, the split should sit on the deal and flow into the payout ledger, not live in a spreadsheet on someone's desktop.   2. Add owner and handoff fields to the deal record
  3. Create stages that mark each transfer point
  4. Set a time limit for every handoff, such as same business day
  5. Review stuck handoffs in the daily huddle until the habit sticks

Questions from the floor

When should a full-cycle shop switch to openers and closers?

Watch new-lead contact time. When brokers are so busy chasing files and stipulations that fresh leads wait hours for a first call, the full-cycle model is costing you deals. That is usually the moment to pull prospecting into a dedicated opener role. Make the change with one or two volunteers first, write the transfer standard together, and expand only after the handoffs run cleanly for a few weeks.

Who owns the merchant at renewal time?

Decide this before it becomes a dispute. Common approaches are that the funding closer keeps the renewal, that a dedicated account manager takes over after funding, or that renewals return to the pod. Any of these can work. What causes friction is leaving it undefined. Put the rule in writing, record the owner on the deal, and apply it consistently so brokers trust the system.

Do processors need to be licensed brokers?

Requirements vary by state and by the exact tasks a processor performs, and they change over time. Some shops keep processors strictly on document collection and lender packaging, with no rate or offer conversations with merchants. Confirm the current rules that apply to your shop and to each role with qualified counsel before you define responsibilities, and record the decision in your role descriptions.

How do pods share leads fairly?

Use a routing rule that everyone can see, such as round-robin by lead source, assignment by vertical, or capacity-based routing that pauses a pod when its open pipeline passes a set size. Review distribution weekly. Fairness matters less than predictability: brokers accept almost any rule that is applied the same way every day.
