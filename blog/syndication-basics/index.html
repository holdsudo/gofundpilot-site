Syndication Basics for Funders and Brokers

Syndication lets a funder share a deal with other capital providers. It only works when everyone can see the same numbers, from first payment to final payoff.

A funder approves a $100,000 deal but would rather not carry the whole thing alone. A few relationships, perhaps a partner shop and a couple of private investors, are willing to take a share. Within a week, the deal funds with four parties behind it, each entitled to a slice of every payment the merchant makes.

That is syndication in its simplest form. It spreads risk, lets funders write larger or more numerous deals, and gives investors access to the asset class. It also creates an accounting and communication burden that grows with every participant and every payment. Shops that handle it well tend to share one trait: a single source of truth that every party can trust.

Pre-flight summary

  • A syndicated deal has a lead who services it and participants who share pro-rata.
  • The arithmetic is simple; keeping records consistent across payments is the hard part.
  • Define fees, returned payments, payoff discounts and defaults in writing before funding.
  • Investors need regular, complete statements and early notice of problems.
  • Brokers who participate in deals should be open about that conflict.

Leg 01The moving parts of a syndicated deal

Every syndicated deal has a lead, usually the funder that originated, underwrote and contracted with the merchant, and one or more participants who contribute a share of the funding amount. The lead typically services the deal: collecting payments, handling merchant contact and managing any problems.

Participants receive their share of collections, usually less agreed fees, and bear their share of losses if the merchant stops paying. The exact terms, including fees, how losses and recoveries are handled and what decisions participants can influence, come from the participation agreement, which varies from shop to shop.

  • Lead funder: originates, underwrites, contracts and services the deal
  • Participants: contribute capital for a defined share
  • Participation percentage: each party's share of the funding and of collections
  • Fees: servicing or management fees the lead may deduct, per the agreement
  • Payoff and default handling: how final payments, discounts and losses are shared

Leg 02Pro-rata, worked through once

Pro-rata means each party receives collections in proportion to what it contributed. Here is a hypothetical deal; the figures are illustrative only, not typical terms or expected results.

A $100,000 advance with a total payback of $135,000. The lead funds 60 percent ($60,000), Participant A funds 25 percent ($25,000), and Participant B funds 15 percent ($15,000). The agreement provides for a servicing fee of 5 percent of collections, kept by the lead.

Say the merchant's payments in a given week total $5,000. The 5 percent fee is $250, leaving $4,750 to distribute. Participant A receives 25 percent of that, $1,187.50. Participant B receives 15 percent, $712.50. The lead's own 60 percent share is $2,850, plus the $250 fee.

Simple on one payment. Across hundreds of daily payments, multiple deals per investor, a returned payment or two and the occasional settlement, the arithmetic is still simple but the bookkeeping is not. That is where most syndication friction comes from. Recording participation percentages and fee terms once, on the deal in FundPilot, means every later distribution is calculated from the same inputs instead of retyped.

The math of syndication is simple. Keeping it correct across thousands of payments is the actual work.

Leg 03Where syndication goes wrong

The common failures are rarely about the deal itself. They are about records drifting apart. The lead's spreadsheet says one thing, the investor's statement says another, and a returned payment from three weeks ago was reversed in one place and not the other.

Other trouble spots include unclear treatment of early payoffs, where the merchant pays a discounted amount and participants are not sure how the discount was shared, and defaults, where investors hear about a problem only when distributions stop.

  • Returned or reversed payments not reflected in investor balances
  • Fees applied inconsistently across participants
  • Payoff discounts shared without clear documentation
  • Late notice to investors when a merchant stops paying
  • Separate spreadsheets for collections, distributions and commissions that never fully agree

Leg 04Reporting that investors can rely on

Investor confidence rests on reporting that is regular, consistent and complete. A good statement shows each deal the investor participates in, their contribution, collections to date, fees deducted, distributions paid and the remaining expected balance. It also shows status: performing, slow, in reconciliation, in default or paid off.

Bad news belongs in the report as early as good news. If a merchant has requested a reconciliation or payments have been returned, participants should hear it from the lead promptly, along with what the lead is doing about it.

FundPilot handles participations, payment schedules, payoffs and broker payouts on one ledger, and gives investors a role with access limited to their own positions. That means an investor sees the same figures the lead sees for their share, without the lead preparing statements by hand or exposing other parties' data.

Leg 05Setting up a syndicate cleanly

Most problems can be prevented at setup. Before the first syndicated deal funds, agree the terms in writing and decide how they will be recorded so every later calculation follows the same rules.

Syndication arrangements can raise legal and regulatory questions, including how investor participation is structured and documented. Have participation agreements prepared or reviewed by qualified counsel; this article is general information, not legal advice.

  1. Put each participant's terms in a written participation agreement.
  2. Record the participation percentage and fee terms on the deal before funding.
  3. Decide how returned payments, payoff discounts and recoveries will be allocated.
  4. Set a distribution schedule and stick to it.
  5. Give each investor access to their own positions and statements.
  6. Reconcile collections, distributions and fees on a fixed schedule, not only at month end.

Leg 06What brokers should understand about it

Brokers do not usually manage syndicates, but syndication affects them. A funder's appetite for larger files can depend on whether it has participants lined up. A funder whose syndicate reporting is messy may also be slow to confirm balances for your renewals.

Some ISOs and brokers participate in the deals they place. That can align incentives, but it also creates a conflict worth being open about, both with the funder and, where relevant, in how you present offers to the merchant. Your recommendation should never depend on whether you hold a share of the deal.

Questions from the floor

What is a participation in MCA syndication?

A participation is a share of a funded deal that a capital provider takes alongside the lead funder. The participant contributes part of the funding amount and receives the same proportion of collections, usually after agreed fees, while also sharing proportionally in any losses. The specific rights and obligations are set out in a participation agreement, which differs between funders and should be reviewed carefully.

Are syndicate returns guaranteed?

No. Participants share in the deal's performance, which depends on the merchant's business and payments. If a merchant's sales fall or payments stop, participants receive less than expected and may lose part of their contribution. Any responsible lead should describe this risk plainly to investors and report problems promptly, rather than presenting expected collections as a certainty.

How often should syndicate investors receive statements?

That should be set in the participation agreement, and it varies. Whatever the schedule, statements should be consistent, show every deal, collection, fee and distribution, and flag status changes such as reconciliations or defaults. Many leads also give investors direct access to their positions between statements, which reduces questions and builds trust because the numbers are visible at any time.
