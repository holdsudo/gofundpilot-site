True Revenue vs. Total Deposits: Removing the Noise

The gap between what hit the account and what the business actually earned is where most quoted offers go wrong. Here is how to close it before a funder does.

You told the merchant they were looking at roughly $60,000 a month. Two days later the funder came back calculating about $44,000, and the offer was a third smaller than the merchant expected. Nobody lied. The statements simply carried money that was never revenue, and the funder removed it while you did not.

Underwriters call this scrubbing the statements. The phrase sounds technical, but the work is ordinary: identify each credit that did not come from selling goods or services, take it out, and keep a note of why. Do it before you quote anything and your numbers will survive review far more often.

Below are the five kinds of noise that show up most, how to recognise each, and how to present what is left.

Pre-flight summary

  • Total deposits almost always overstate revenue; quote only after removing non-revenue credits.
  • Internal transfers and other funding proceeds are the two biggest sources of inflation.
  • Cash is revenue when it is regular and fits the business; one-off cash needs an explanation.
  • Show your adjustments line by line so a lender can verify them quickly.
  • Conservative, explained numbers protect both the merchant and your lender relationships.

Leg 01Noise type one: money moving between the merchant's own accounts

Internal transfers are the most common inflation. A restaurant owner moves $8,000 from a savings account to cover payroll, or sweeps money between two operating accounts. On the statement these look like deposits. They are not new money, and a funder will remove them.

Look for descriptors such as online transfer, from savings, or a partial account number. Round amounts that match a debit in another account the merchant owns are a strong hint. If the merchant has two accounts, ask for both sets of statements so you can match each transfer to its source and avoid counting the same dollars twice.

Leg 02Noise type two: proceeds from other funding

When a merchant has taken a previous advance or a term loan from a bank, the funded amount lands as a large single credit. It can make one month look extraordinary. It is not revenue; it is capital that will be paid back, often through the very debits you will be counting as existing positions.

These credits usually carry the provider's name or a wire reference and arrive as one lump. Remove them from revenue, then follow the trail: a funding deposit almost always has a matching recurring debit starting soon after. Finding one helps you find the other, and both belong in your notes.

A large one-time credit is a question to ask the merchant, never a number to quote.

Leg 03Noise type three: refunds, reversals and chargebacks

A vendor refund, an insurance reimbursement, or a reversed payment the business made earlier all arrive as credits. So do returned items, when a payment the business tried to make bounces back. None of these reflect sales.

On the other side, card chargebacks reduce what the business keeps from sales it already recorded. If chargebacks are frequent, mention them in your summary, since they say something about the business that the deposit total hides.

  • Vendor or supplier refunds
  • Insurance or tax refunds
  • Reversed debits and returned items
  • Loan or lease refunds after a payoff
  • Duplicate credits later reversed by the bank

Leg 04Noise type four: owner injections and cash

Owners sometimes put personal money into the business before applying for funding, sometimes to cover a gap and sometimes, unhelpfully, to make the account look stronger. Either way, it is not revenue. Credits from the owner's personal account, or round deposits that appear just before the statement period ends, deserve a direct question.

Cash deposits are more nuanced. For a bar, salon or food truck, cash is genuine sales and removing it would understate the business. The usual approach is to check whether cash deposits are regular and consistent with the type of business. Steady weekly cash deposits fit a cash-heavy business. A single large cash deposit in an otherwise card-only account does not, and you should ask what it was.

Repeat for every month in the file, then look at the trend rather than any one month.   1. Start with total deposits: $61,200.
  2. Subtract a transfer from the owner's savings: $7,000.
  3. Subtract proceeds from a prior funding provider: $9,500.
  4. Subtract a parts supplier refund: $1,350.
  5. Keep weekly cash deposits that match prior months: no change.
  6. Record true revenue of $43,350 with each adjustment noted.

Leg 06Presenting adjusted revenue without shading it

Some brokers are tempted to leave a borderline item in because it helps the numbers. Resist that. The funder will find it, and the next file you send will get a closer look. Presenting conservative, explained figures builds the kind of lender trust that matters more over a year than any single deal.

The same honesty helps the merchant. An offer sized on real revenue is an offer the business is more likely to be able to carry. Sizing to inflated deposits risks a remittance that strains the account within weeks, which is bad for the merchant and bad for your renewals. Keep your adjustment notes on the deal record in FundPilot alongside the statements, so whoever handles the renewal months later can see exactly how the original numbers were built.

Questions from the floor

Do all funders calculate true revenue the same way?

No. Funders apply their own rules, and some are stricter about cash or certain transfer types than others. Your job is not to guess each funder's formula but to give them a clear starting point: total deposits, each item you removed, and why. That way they can apply their own method quickly, and any differences are easy to discuss rather than a surprise.

What if I cannot tell whether a deposit is revenue?

Ask the merchant and record the answer on the deal. If it remains unclear, present the figure both ways, with and without the item, and flag it in your notes. Funders generally prefer an honest question mark to a confident number that later turns out to be wrong. Supporting documents, such as an invoice for a large customer payment, can settle it.

Should owner injections ever count toward revenue?

Generally no, because they are the owner's money moving into the business, not income from customers. They can still be useful context: an owner who regularly supports the business with personal funds is telling you something about cash flow. Mention them plainly in your summary rather than burying them, and never suggest a merchant deposit personal money to improve their statements.
